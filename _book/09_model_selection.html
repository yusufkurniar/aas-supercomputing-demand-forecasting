10  Model Selection

10.1 Purpose and Approach

This chapter selects the forecasting models and analytical assumptions that will inform projections of Australian national research high-performance computing (HPC) demand to 2030.

The selection process builds on the candidate models developed in Chapter 07 and the historical forecast evaluation conducted in Chapter 08. Overall HPC demand, CPU demand, and GPU demand are considered separately because they have different measurement units, historical coverage, and forecasting uncertainties.

Model selection is guided by predictive performance, statistical adequacy, interpretability, and the reliability of the underlying data. Particular attention is given to the substantial increase in recorded overall HPC demand in 2026 and whether this observation should influence the central forecast or be examined through sensitivity scenarios.

Field of Research (FoR) allocation shares are assessed separately. These shares describe the historical distribution of allocated resources rather than directly observed research-field demand. They are therefore used to inform scenario assumptions, not to select independently validated field-specific demand models.

10.2 Model Selection Criteria

The following criteria are used to assess the candidate forecasting models.

  1. Out-of-sample forecast accuracy: Rolling-origin cross-validation results from Chapter 08 provide the principal quantitative evidence. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) are evaluated within each resource series, rather than compared directly across different measurement units.

  2. Consistency across evaluation metrics: Models are assessed using both RMSE and MAE. RMSE places greater weight on large forecast errors, while MAE describes the average absolute forecast error. Differences between their rankings are considered when interpreting model performance.

  3. Statistical adequacy: Residual behaviour and evidence of systematic forecast errors are considered where supported by the available data. Diagnostic results are interpreted cautiously because the annual series contain relatively few observations.

  4. Interpretability and plausibility: Selected models should provide transparent assumptions about future demand growth and produce forecasts that can be explained in the context of national research infrastructure planning.

  5. Historical data quality and coverage: Model complexity is assessed relative to the length, consistency, and provenance of each resource series. Short or partly estimated historical series do not necessarily support complex forecasting models.

  6. Sensitivity to recent observations: The influence of the 2026 demand increase is considered when interpreting fitted trends and selecting models. The increase is not automatically assumed to represent a permanent acceleration in demand.

  7. Suitability for projections to 2030: The selected models must support interpretable forecasts over the 2027–2030 horizon. Forecast uncertainty and alternative growth assumptions will be examined in Chapter 10.

No single criterion determines model selection automatically. Out-of-sample accuracy provides the primary quantitative comparison, while the remaining criteria support a transparent assessment of model suitability for policy-oriented forecasting.

10.3 Overall HPC Demand Model Selection

10.3.1 Comparison of Candidate Models

Four candidate models were evaluated for overall HPC demand: linear trend, log-linear trend, drift, and naïve forecasting. Their predictive performance was assessed in Chapter 08 using expanding-window, one-step-ahead rolling-origin cross-validation.

The initial training period covered 2014 - 2020, followed by six evaluation years from 2021 to 2026. Each model was re-estimated using the observations available before the corresponding forecast year, avoiding the use of future observations in model fitting.

Model RMSE (kSU) MAE (kSU) RMSE Rank
Linear trend 290,898 198,295 1
Drift 314,588 221,778 2
Naïve 378,864 283,000 3
Log-linear trend 511,614 466,991 4

The linear trend model achieved the lowest RMSE and MAE, indicating the strongest historical one-step-ahead predictive performance among the evaluated candidates. Its RMSE was approximately 7.5% lower than the drift model and 23.2% lower than the naïve benchmark.

The agreement between RMSE and MAE rankings provides additional support for the linear trend model. In contrast, the log-linear trend model produced substantially larger forecast errors, suggesting that its proportional-growth assumption was less effective over the historical evaluation period.

These findings support the linear trend model as the leading candidate for the central overall HPC demand forecast. However, one-step-ahead performance does not guarantee equivalent accuracy over the longer 2027 - 2030 forecasting horizon. The final selection must also consider the sensitivity of fitted trends to recent observations and the interpretation of historical demand measurements.

10.3.2 Sensitivity to the 2026 Observation

The 2026 observation warrants particular attention when selecting a model for overall HPC demand. Recorded demand increased from 1,564,643 kSU in 2025 to 2,242,817 kSU in 2026, representing an increase of approximately 43.3%.

Over the same period, recorded allocations decreased slightly from 876,200 kSU to 872,288 kSU. Consequently, the ratio of allocated resources to recorded demand declined from approximately 56.0% to 38.9%. This divergence highlights the importance of distinguishing requested HPC resources from the resources ultimately allocated.

The increase in recorded demand may reflect changes in computational requirements, the composition of applications, or other factors affecting the reporting of requested resources. However, the available analysis does not establish the relative contribution of these explanations. The increase should therefore not be attributed to a specific technological or institutional development without supporting evidence.

10.3.2.1 Implications for Trend Estimation

The candidate models developed in Chapter 07 include trend specifications estimated using the available historical observations, alongside sensitivity analysis examining the influence of the latest observation.

Including 2026 allows the fitted models to incorporate the most recent reported demand. However, a substantial increase near the end of a short annual series can influence estimated trend coefficients and subsequent forecasts.

Excluding 2026 provides a sensitivity comparison showing how projected demand changes when the latest observation does not influence model estimation. This comparison is not intended to imply that the 2026 observation is incorrect or should be discarded.

The two estimation periods therefore serve different analytical purposes:

  • Including 2026: Represents a model fitted to the complete available historical series.
  • Excluding 2026: Provides a sensitivity benchmark for assessing the influence of the latest demand increase.

The preferred model should not be determined solely by whether its forecast is higher or lower. Instead, the comparison should consider historical predictive performance, the plausibility of extrapolated growth, and the uncertainty associated with the most recent observation.

10.3.2.2 Interpretation for Model Selection

The rolling-origin evaluation in Chapter 08 identified the linear trend as the strongest candidate for overall HPC demand based on both RMSE and MAE.

Nevertheless, this evaluation primarily assesses one-step-ahead forecast performance. It does not independently establish the reliability of forecasts over the full 2027–2030 horizon, particularly following a substantial increase in the final observed year.

Accordingly, the linear trend remains the leading candidate for the central overall HPC demand forecast, while the sensitivity of projections to the inclusion of 2026 must be considered before finalising the forecasting assumptions.

The resulting differences in projected demand will inform the uncertainty and alternative-growth scenarios developed in Chapter 10.

10.3.3 Overall HPC Model Selection Decision

Based on the rolling-origin evaluation and sensitivity analysis, the linear trend model fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series is selected as the central statistical forecasting specification for overall HPC demand.

This selection is supported by three considerations.

First, the linear trend achieved the lowest out-of-sample RMSE (290,898 kSU) and MAE (198,295 kSU) among the four candidate models evaluated in Chapter 08. It therefore provides the strongest historical one-step-ahead predictive performance within the tested model set.

Second, the linear specification assumes a constant absolute annual increase rather than sustained compounded percentage growth. This produces a more conservative and interpretable extrapolation than the log-linear model, which generated substantially larger forecast errors and much higher long-horizon projections.

Third, the model remains sensitive to the inclusion of the latest observation, but this sensitivity can be quantified and communicated transparently.

The full-history linear model estimates an annual increase of approximately 161,992 kSU and projects overall HPC demand of approximately 2.50 million kSU in 2030.

When the model is fitted using observations from 2014–2025, the estimated annual increase decreases to approximately 144,357 kSU, producing a 2030 projection of approximately 2.29 million kSU.

The difference between these projections demonstrates the influence of the 2026 observation on the estimated trend. It does not represent a formal prediction interval or establish a statistically validated lower bound for future demand.

The following specifications are therefore retained for subsequent analysis:

  • Central statistical projection: Linear trend fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series.
  • Historical sensitivity comparison: Linear trend fitted to 2014–2025, excluding the latest observation.
  • Alternative growth assumption: Log-linear trend, retained as an exploratory illustration of compounded growth rather than a preferred forecasting model.

The central projection should be interpreted as a model-based extrapolation of recorded and harmonised HPC demand, not a definitive estimate of unconstrained future researcher requirements.

The selected model and sensitivity comparisons will inform the development of explicit demand scenarios in Chapter 10. These scenarios will distinguish modelling assumptions from statistical forecast uncertainty and acknowledge limitations in historical data comparability.

10.4 CPU Demand Model Selection

10.4.1 Comparison of Candidate Models

CPU demand was evaluated separately from overall HPC demand to account for differences in resource measurement, historical coverage, and computational requirements.

Four candidate forecasting models were compared: linear trend, log-linear trend, drift, and naïve forecasting.

As described in Chapter 08, model performance was assessed using expanding-window, one-step-ahead rolling-origin cross-validation, with an initial training period of 2014–2020 and six evaluation years from 2021 to 2026.

CPU demand is expressed in millions of core-hours.

Model RMSE (million core-hours) MAE (million core-hours) RMSE Rank
Linear trend 157.72 138.65 1
Log-linear trend 160.18 133.74 2
Drift 242.61 193.69 3
Naïve 260.05 228.45 4

The linear trend model achieved the lowest RMSE of 157.72 million core-hours, approximately 1.5% lower than the log-linear trend model.

However, the log-linear model achieved the lowest MAE of 133.74 million core-hours, compared with 138.65 million core-hours for the linear model.

Unlike the overall HPC evaluation, the two accuracy measures therefore produce different rankings for the leading CPU models.

This distinction is important because RMSE places greater weight on larger forecast errors, whereas MAE measures the average magnitude of errors without additional quadratic weighting.

The relatively small difference in RMSE suggests that the linear and log-linear models have comparable historical one-step-ahead forecasting performance. Consequently, selecting the preferred CPU model requires consideration of the implied growth assumptions and their consequences for longer-term projections.

10.4.2 Assessment of Growth Assumptions

The linear trend model assumes a constant absolute annual increase in CPU demand, while the log-linear trend model assumes approximately constant proportional growth.

Chapter 07 demonstrated that these assumptions generate substantially different projections for 2030.

The linear specifications project CPU demand between approximately 1,263.85 and 1,369.90 million core-hours in 2030, depending on whether the 2026 observation is included.

By comparison, the log-linear specifications project approximately 2,479.16 to 2,502.07 million core-hours.

The considerably higher log-linear projections arise from the compounding of proportional growth over the forecast horizon.

Although the log-linear model performs slightly better on MAE, the historical evaluation does not provide sufficiently strong evidence that compounded growth will persist through 2030.

The linear trend is therefore more suitable as the leading candidate for a transparent central statistical projection, while the log-linear specification remains useful for examining alternative growth assumptions.

These projections are model-based trajectories rather than statistical prediction intervals or independently validated demand scenarios.

10.4.3 CPU Model Selection Decision

The linear trend model fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series is selected as the central statistical forecasting specification for CPU demand.

This decision reflects the combined assessment of historical forecast accuracy, model interpretability, and the implications of extrapolating alternative growth assumptions through 2030.

The linear model achieved the lowest rolling-origin RMSE of 157.72 million core-hours, compared with 160.18 million core-hours for the log-linear model. However, the log-linear model achieved a slightly lower MAE of 133.74 million core-hours, compared with 138.65 million core-hours for the linear model.

These differences are relatively small and do not establish a statistically significant advantage for either model. Both specifications therefore remain plausible candidates based on their historical one-step-ahead forecasting performance.

The principal distinction lies in their growth assumptions. The linear model extrapolates an approximately constant absolute increase in CPU demand, whereas the log-linear model extrapolates compounded proportional growth.

Chapter 07 showed that these assumptions produce substantially different 2030 projections. The linear specifications generate approximately 1.26–1.37 billion core-hours, while the log-linear specifications generate approximately 2.48–2.50 billion core-hours.

Given the short annual series, differences in historical measurement and conversion assumptions, and uncertainty regarding the persistence of recent demand growth, the evidence is insufficient to establish compounded growth as the most appropriate central forecasting assumption.

The linear model is therefore preferred for the central statistical projection because it provides competitive historical forecast accuracy while retaining a simpler and more transparent growth assumption.

The following specifications are retained:

  • Central statistical projection: Linear trend fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series.
  • Historical sensitivity comparison: Linear trend fitted to 2014–2025, excluding the latest observation.
  • Alternative growth assumption: Log-linear trend, retained to examine the implications of sustained proportional growth.

The 2026-excluded linear specification provides a sensitivity comparison rather than an independently validated lower bound. Similarly, the log-linear specification represents an alternative growth trajectory, not a statistically established upper bound.

The selected CPU model should be interpreted as a projection of the harmonised historical CPU demand measure, subject to the provenance, conversion, and reporting limitations documented in earlier chapters.

Chapter 10 will use these specifications to examine alternative CPU demand trajectories through 2030 without treating differences between models as formal prediction intervals.

10.5 GPU Demand Model Selection

10.5.1 Comparison of Candidate Models

GPU demand was evaluated separately from overall HPC and CPU demand because of its shorter historical coverage, differences in data provenance, and greater uncertainty surrounding the measurement of computational requirements.

The GPU forecasting models developed in Chapter 07 use five annual demand estimates covering 2022–2026. These estimates combine reported Setonix-based demand information with values derived using allocation shares and working conversion assumptions.

Four candidate models were assessed in Chapter 08: naïve forecasting, drift, linear trend, and log-linear trend.

An expanding-window, one-step-ahead rolling-origin evaluation was conducted using the available recent-period observations. Unlike the overall HPC and CPU evaluations, the GPU comparison contains only two test years, 2025 and 2026.

Model RMSE (million GPU-hours) MAE (million GPU-hours) RMSE Rank
Naïve 0.441 0.353 1
Drift 0.537 0.490 2
Log-linear trend 0.719 0.715 3
Linear trend 0.729 0.725 4

The naïve model achieved the lowest RMSE and MAE among the evaluated candidates. It therefore provided the strongest historical one-step-ahead predictive performance within this limited evaluation.

However, the comparison is based on only two forecast errors per model. Consequently, the resulting accuracy rankings may be strongly influenced by individual observations and should not be interpreted as robust evidence of long-term predictive superiority.

The naïve model assumes that future GPU demand remains at the most recently observed level. Although this assumption performed comparatively well during the evaluation period, it does not establish that GPU demand will remain constant through 2030.

Similarly, the higher forecast errors recorded by the linear and log-linear models do not establish that future GPU demand growth is absent. They indicate that these trend specifications were less accurate over the two available historical test years.

The results therefore support the naïve model as a short-term statistical benchmark, rather than a definitive long-term forecasting model.

10.5.2 Sensitivity to the 2026 Observation

Chapter 07 examined the sensitivity of GPU trend estimates to the inclusion of the 2026 observation.

For the linear trend specification, the estimated annual increase changed from approximately 0.012 million GPU-hours when fitted to 2022–2025 to approximately 0.172 million GPU-hours when 2026 was included.

The log-linear trend coefficient also increased, from approximately 0.020 to 0.081.

These changes demonstrate that the estimated GPU growth trajectory is highly sensitive to the latest observation. However, the large relative percentage changes partly reflect the small trend coefficients estimated from the shorter 2022–2025 sample.

The resulting 2030 projections also differ substantially between specifications.

The linear trend models produce projected GPU demand ranging from approximately 2.252 to 3.371 million GPU-hours, while the log-linear models produce projections ranging from approximately 2.406 to 3.681 million GPU-hours.

These values represent alternative model-based trajectories, not formal prediction intervals or statistically validated lower and upper demand bounds.

The short modelling period and mixture of reported and estimated demand values limit confidence in extrapolating any single trend through 2030.

Furthermore, the historical GPU conversion scenarios developed in Chapter 05 represent uncertainty associated with conversion assumptions. They should not be interpreted as additional observed GPU demand data or as independent evidence of future growth.

Consequently, the GPU model selection process must distinguish between short-term predictive performance, sensitivity to individual observations, and the broader uncertainty surrounding future GPU requirements.

10.5.3 GPU Model Selection Decision

The naïve forecasting model is retained as the preferred statistical benchmark for GPU demand because it achieved the lowest RMSE (0.441 million GPU-hours) and MAE (0.353 million GPU-hours) in the rolling-origin evaluation conducted in Chapter 08.

However, no single GPU forecasting model is selected as a sufficiently validated central projection through 2030.

This decision reflects three considerations.

First, the evaluation contains only two one-step-ahead test observations, covering 2025 and 2026. Although the naïve model performed best within this comparison, the limited number of forecast errors does not provide robust evidence that it will remain the most accurate model over a four-year forecasting horizon.

Second, the GPU modelling series contains only five annual estimates from 2022–2026. These estimates combine reported Setonix-based demand information with values derived using allocation shares and working conversion assumptions. Differences in data provenance limit the comparability of observations and the reliability of estimated growth trends.

Third, the linear and log-linear specifications are highly sensitive to the inclusion of 2026. Chapter 07 demonstrated substantial changes in their estimated trend coefficients and resulting 2030 projections when the latest observation was included or excluded.

The naïve model is therefore retained as a reference trajectory representing unchanged demand relative to the latest observation. It should not be interpreted as evidence that future GPU requirements will remain constant.

The linear and log-linear trend models are retained as exploratory growth trajectories. These specifications illustrate how projected demand changes under alternative assumptions about absolute and proportional growth, but their historical performance and sensitivity do not justify selecting either as a definitive long-term forecast.

The following approach is adopted for subsequent scenario analysis:

  • Statistical reference benchmark: Naïve model, representing unchanged GPU demand from the latest observation.
  • Exploratory absolute-growth trajectory: Linear trend fitted to the available 2022–2026 estimates.
  • Exploratory proportional-growth trajectory: Log-linear trend fitted to the available 2022–2026 estimates.
  • Historical sensitivity comparisons: Alternative trend specifications excluding 2026, used to assess dependence on the latest observation.
  • Conversion uncertainty: Historical GPU conversion scenarios retained separately from future growth assumptions.

These trajectories are not assigned probabilities or interpreted as statistically validated lower, central, and upper demand bounds.

Consequently, the GPU analysis provides an exploratory assessment of possible future resource requirements rather than a definitive forecast of national research GPU demand.

Chapter 10 will present these alternatives transparently, distinguishing historical measurement uncertainty from assumptions about future demand growth.

10.6 Field of Research Scenario Assumptions

10.6.1 Purpose and Analytical Approach

Field of Research (FoR) is incorporated into the forecasting framework to examine how projected national HPC demand could be distributed across research disciplines under alternative allocation-composition assumptions.

Unlike overall HPC, CPU, and GPU demand, the harmonised FoR dataset primarily represents historically allocated resources rather than directly observed requests for computational resources within individual research fields.

Consequently, FoR-specific demand cannot be independently forecast and validated using the same approach applied to the aggregate resource series.

Instead, a top-down scenario framework is adopted. Under this approach, projected overall HPC demand is distributed across harmonised FoR categories using explicit assumptions about future allocation shares.

These estimates represent hypothetical distributions of projected demand based on historical allocation patterns, rather than independently validated forecasts of researcher demand within each field.

10.6.2 Historical Evidence for Scenario Assumptions

Chapter 08 evaluated harmonised FoR allocation shares across 2014–2026, including separate assessments of the earlier 2014–2020 and recent 2021–2026 periods.

During 2021–2026, Engineering & Technology accounted for the largest average allocation share (34.88%), followed by Physical Sciences (22.13%), Chemical Sciences (17.84%), and Earth & Environmental Sciences (14.00%).

The longer historical comparison identified substantial differences in recorded allocation composition between reporting periods.

Engineering & Technology increased from an average allocation share of 16.90% in 2014–2020 to 34.88% in 2021–2026, representing a difference of +17.98 percentage points.

Conversely, Earth & Environmental Sciences decreased from 31.62% to 14.00%, a difference of -17.61 percentage points.

Physical Sciences increased by 5.21 percentage points, while Mathematics & Computing decreased by 2.46 percentage points.

These changes demonstrate that the recorded distribution of HPC allocations has varied over time. However, they cannot be interpreted exclusively as changes in underlying researcher demand because the historical series spans different FoR classification standards, reporting coverage, and allocation practices.

The observed differences therefore provide evidence for exploring alternative allocation-composition assumptions, but not for extrapolating discipline-specific demand growth rates directly.

10.6.3 Selection of FoR Scenario Assumptions

Three allocation-composition approaches are proposed for scenario development in Chapter 10.

Recent-period composition

The first approach uses the harmonised allocation-share patterns observed during 2021–2026 as a reference for a contemporary allocation-composition scenario.

This approach reflects the more recent reporting framework and provides a transparent historical basis for distributing projected overall HPC demand across research fields.

However, the observed annual shares are not constant, and some categories are missing from particular years. The implementation must therefore define how category coverage, missing observations, and share normalisation are handled.

Latest-year composition

The second approach uses the 2026 allocation-share distribution as an alternative reference.

This approach illustrates the implications of maintaining the latest observed composition throughout the projection horizon.

However, a single year’s allocation shares may reflect temporary conditions or reporting-specific effects. The 2026 composition should therefore not automatically be considered more representative of future researcher demand than the recent-period average.

Historical-composition sensitivity

The third approach uses the earlier 2014–2020 allocation composition to examine how projected demand distributions change under a different historical allocation pattern.

This approach is intended as a sensitivity comparison rather than a direct prediction that earlier allocation patterns will return.

Differences in FoR classification frameworks, reporting scope, and category coverage must be considered before constructing comparable scenario shares.

10.6.4 Interpretation and Limitations

The proposed FoR scenarios are conditional on two distinct assumptions:

  1. The selected overall HPC model provides an indicative trajectory of total national research HPC demand.
  2. Historical FoR allocation shares provide a reasonable reference for constructing hypothetical distributions of that projected demand.

Neither assumption establishes that the resulting FoR-level estimates represent directly observed or independently validated future researcher demand.

The allocation shares must also form a complete and internally consistent distribution for each scenario. Missing categories should not automatically be treated as zero allocations, and any normalisation or category exclusions must be documented.

Furthermore, changes in allocation composition may reflect resource constraints, institutional priorities, facility coverage, or reporting conventions rather than changes in unconstrained computational requirements.

For these reasons, FoR scenarios will be presented as allocation-share-based distributions of projected overall demand, not as independent field-specific demand forecasts.

Chapter 10 will implement these assumptions and examine how alternative historical compositions affect the indicative distribution of HPC requirements through 2030.

10.7 Selected Models and Limitations

10.7.1 Summary of Model Selection Decisions

The forecasting models and analytical assumptions selected in this chapter are summarised below.

CPU Full-history linear trend Lowest RMSE; competitive MAE across six evaluation years Central statistical projection

FoR Historical allocation-share assumptions Descriptive stability and period-comparison analysis Conditional distributions of projected overall HPC demand

The selected approaches reflect differences in data availability, predictive evidence, and the interpretation of each demand measure.

For overall HPC, the linear trend provides the strongest historical one-step-ahead forecasting performance among the evaluated models. Its full-history specification is selected as the central statistical projection, while the 2026-excluded specification is retained for sensitivity analysis.

For CPU demand, the linear trend is also selected as the central statistical projection. Although the log-linear model achieved a slightly lower MAE, the linear model achieved the lowest RMSE and avoids assuming that historical proportional growth will necessarily continue through 2030.

However, the short modelling period, limited evaluation sample, and sensitivity of trend estimates to 2026 prevent the selection of a sufficiently validated central long-term forecast. For FoR, no independent demand forecasting model is selected. Instead, historical allocation-share patterns are used to construct conditional distributions of projected overall HPC demand across research fields.

10.7.2 Forecasting Uncertainty and Data Limitations

Several limitations apply across the selected forecasting approaches.

Historical measurement and comparability

The harmonised historical series combine directly reported observations, derived estimates, and proxy-based values. Differences in resource accounting, reporting coverage, and conversion assumptions may affect comparability across years.

Consequently, the resulting forecasts should be interpreted as projections of the constructed historical demand measures rather than definitive estimates of unconstrained national researcher requirements.

Limited annual observations

The overall HPC and CPU forecasting series contain 13 annual observations covering 2014–2026.

Although rolling-origin cross-validation provides useful out-of-sample evidence, the six available evaluation years limit the precision with which differences in predictive performance can be established.

The GPU series contains only five recent annual estimates and two evaluation years, resulting in substantially greater model-selection uncertainty.

Sensitivity to recent demand changes

The substantial increase in recorded overall HPC demand in 2026 affects estimated historical trends and projected future demand.

The influence of this observation is examined using alternative specifications that exclude 2026. These comparisons assess sensitivity but do not establish whether the observed increase represents a temporary change, a sustained acceleration, or differences in reporting and measurement.

Long-horizon extrapolation

The rolling-origin evaluations primarily assess one-step-ahead predictive performance.

Forecasts extending to 2030 require additional assumptions about the persistence of historical trends. Strong performance over a one-year horizon does not guarantee equivalent performance over four years.

Resource-specific interpretation

Overall HPC demand is expressed in kSU, CPU demand in core-hours, and GPU demand in GPU-hours.

These measures describe different aspects of computing requirements and should not be directly added or compared without an explicitly justified conversion framework.

FoR allocation-share interpretation

FoR allocation shares represent the recorded distribution of allocated resources rather than directly observed field-specific demand.

The resulting scenarios depend on assumptions about how historical allocation composition may relate to the future distribution of computational requirements. They are not independently validated FoR demand forecasts.

10.7.3 Implications for Scenario Development

The model-selection findings establish the analytical basis for Chapter 10.

The full-history linear trend models will provide central statistical projections for overall HPC and CPU demand, with 2026-excluded specifications used to examine sensitivity to the latest observation.

Alternative log-linear trajectories will illustrate the implications of compounded proportional growth. These alternatives will not automatically be interpreted as statistically established upper demand bounds.

For GPU demand, the naïve benchmark and exploratory trend specifications will be presented separately, reflecting the limited evidence available for selecting a definitive long-term forecasting model.

For FoR, three historical allocation-share reference periods will be considered: earlier (2014 - 2020), recent (2021 - 2026), and long-term (2014 - 2026). The resulting normalised allocation-share weights will be applied to the central overall HPC demand projection to examine how the illustrative distribution across research fields varies with the selected reference period. These distributions are conditional planning scenarios rather than independently estimated FoR demand forecasts.

Scenario development will distinguish three sources of uncertainty:

  1. Historical measurement uncertainty: Differences in data provenance, reporting scope, and conversion assumptions.
  2. Model uncertainty: Differences between forecasting specifications and their underlying growth assumptions.
  3. Scenario uncertainty: Alternative assumptions about future demand growth and the distribution of computational requirements.

These sources of uncertainty will not be treated as interchangeable.

The scenarios will therefore provide transparent, assumption-dependent projections for national HPC infrastructure planning rather than probabilistic estimates of all possible future outcomes.

10.8 Chapter Summary

This chapter selected forecasting approaches for overall HPC, CPU, and GPU demand using the predictive evaluation results from Chapter 08 and the modelling evidence developed in Chapter 07.

Linear trend models were selected as the central statistical specifications for overall HPC and CPU demand because they provide competitive historical forecast accuracy and interpretable absolute-growth assumptions.

For GPU demand, the naïve model was retained as a statistical benchmark, while alternative trend models were retained for exploratory analysis because of limited historical evidence.

FoR allocation-share assumptions were selected as a framework for distributing projected overall HPC demand across research fields, subject to classification and reporting limitations.

These decisions provide the foundation for the demand scenarios developed in Chapter 10, where alternative growth assumptions, historical sensitivity, and resource-specific uncertainties will be examined through 2030.

The linear trend model achieved the lowest RMSE and MAE, indicating the strongest historical one-step-ahead predictive performance among the evaluated candidates. Its RMSE was approximately 7.5% lower than the drift model and 23.2% lower than the naïve benchmark.

The agreement between RMSE and MAE rankings provides additional support for the linear trend model. In contrast, the log-linear trend model produced substantially larger forecast errors, suggesting that its proportional-growth assumption was less effective over the historical evaluation period.

These findings support the linear trend model as the leading candidate for the central overall HPC demand forecast. However, one-step-ahead performance does not guarantee equivalent accuracy over the longer 2027 - 2030 forecasting horizon. The final selection must also consider the sensitivity of fitted trends to recent observations and the interpretation of historical demand measurements.


### Sensitivity to the 2026 Observation

The 2026 observation warrants particular attention when selecting a model for overall HPC demand. Recorded demand increased from 1,564,643 kSU in 2025 to 2,242,817 kSU in 2026, representing an increase of approximately 43.3%.

Over the same period, recorded allocations decreased slightly from 876,200 kSU to 872,288 kSU. Consequently, the ratio of allocated resources to recorded demand declined from approximately 56.0% to 38.9%. This divergence highlights the importance of distinguishing requested HPC resources from the resources ultimately allocated.

The increase in recorded demand may reflect changes in computational requirements, the composition of applications, or other factors affecting the reporting of requested resources. However, the available analysis does not establish the relative contribution of these explanations. The increase should therefore not be attributed to a specific technological or institutional development without supporting evidence.

#### Implications for Trend Estimation

The candidate models developed in Chapter 07 include trend specifications estimated using the available historical observations, alongside sensitivity analysis examining the influence of the latest observation.

Including 2026 allows the fitted models to incorporate the most recent reported demand. However, a substantial increase near the end of a short annual series can influence estimated trend coefficients and subsequent forecasts.

Excluding 2026 provides a sensitivity comparison showing how projected demand changes when the latest observation does not influence model estimation. This comparison is not intended to imply that the 2026 observation is incorrect or should be discarded.

The two estimation periods therefore serve different analytical purposes:

- **Including 2026:** Represents a model fitted to the complete available historical series.
- **Excluding 2026:** Provides a sensitivity benchmark for assessing the influence of the latest demand increase.

The preferred model should not be determined solely by whether its forecast is higher or lower. Instead, the comparison should consider historical predictive performance, the plausibility of extrapolated growth, and the uncertainty associated with the most recent observation.

#### Interpretation for Model Selection

The rolling-origin evaluation in Chapter 08 identified the linear trend as the strongest candidate for overall HPC demand based on both RMSE and MAE.

Nevertheless, this evaluation primarily assesses one-step-ahead forecast performance. It does not independently establish the reliability of forecasts over the full 2027–2030 horizon, particularly following a substantial increase in the final observed year.

Accordingly, the linear trend remains the leading candidate for the central overall HPC demand forecast, while the sensitivity of projections to the inclusion of 2026 must be considered before finalising the forecasting assumptions.

The resulting differences in projected demand will inform the uncertainty and alternative-growth scenarios developed in Chapter 10.


### Overall HPC Model Selection Decision

Based on the rolling-origin evaluation and sensitivity analysis, the linear trend model fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series is selected as the central statistical forecasting specification for overall HPC demand.

This selection is supported by three considerations.

First, the linear trend achieved the lowest out-of-sample RMSE (290,898 kSU) and MAE (198,295 kSU) among the four candidate models evaluated in Chapter 08. It therefore provides the strongest historical one-step-ahead predictive performance within the tested model set.

Second, the linear specification assumes a constant absolute annual increase rather than sustained compounded percentage growth. This produces a more conservative and interpretable extrapolation than the log-linear model, which generated substantially larger forecast errors and much higher long-horizon projections.

Third, the model remains sensitive to the inclusion of the latest observation, but this sensitivity can be quantified and communicated transparently.

The full-history linear model estimates an annual increase of approximately 161,992 kSU and projects overall HPC demand of approximately 2.50 million kSU in 2030.

When the model is fitted using observations from 2014–2025, the estimated annual increase decreases to approximately 144,357 kSU, producing a 2030 projection of approximately 2.29 million kSU.

The difference between these projections demonstrates the influence of the 2026 observation on the estimated trend. It does not represent a formal prediction interval or establish a statistically validated lower bound for future demand.

The following specifications are therefore retained for subsequent analysis:

- **Central statistical projection:** Linear trend fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series.
- **Historical sensitivity comparison:** Linear trend fitted to 2014–2025, excluding the latest observation.
- **Alternative growth assumption:** Log-linear trend, retained as an exploratory illustration of compounded growth rather than a preferred forecasting model.

The central projection should be interpreted as a model-based extrapolation of recorded and harmonised HPC demand, not a definitive estimate of unconstrained future researcher requirements.

The selected model and sensitivity comparisons will inform the development of explicit demand scenarios in Chapter 10. These scenarios will distinguish modelling assumptions from statistical forecast uncertainty and acknowledge limitations in historical data comparability.


## CPU Demand Model Selection

### Comparison of Candidate Models

CPU demand was evaluated separately from overall HPC demand to account for differences in resource measurement, historical coverage, and computational requirements.

Four candidate forecasting models were compared: linear trend, log-linear trend, drift, and naïve forecasting.

As described in Chapter 08, model performance was assessed using expanding-window, one-step-ahead rolling-origin cross-validation, with an initial training period of 2014–2020 and six evaluation years from 2021 to 2026.

CPU demand is expressed in millions of core-hours.

| Model | RMSE (million core-hours) | MAE (million core-hours) | RMSE Rank |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Linear trend | 157.72 | 138.65 | 1 |
| Log-linear trend | 160.18 | 133.74 | 2 |
| Drift | 242.61 | 193.69 | 3 |
| Naïve | 260.05 | 228.45 | 4 |

The linear trend model achieved the lowest RMSE of 157.72 million core-hours, approximately 1.5% lower than the log-linear trend model.

However, the log-linear model achieved the lowest MAE of 133.74 million core-hours, compared with 138.65 million core-hours for the linear model.

Unlike the overall HPC evaluation, the two accuracy measures therefore produce different rankings for the leading CPU models.

This distinction is important because RMSE places greater weight on larger forecast errors, whereas MAE measures the average magnitude of errors without additional quadratic weighting.

The relatively small difference in RMSE suggests that the linear and log-linear models have comparable historical one-step-ahead forecasting performance. Consequently, selecting the preferred CPU model requires consideration of the implied growth assumptions and their consequences for longer-term projections.

### Assessment of Growth Assumptions

The linear trend model assumes a constant absolute annual increase in CPU demand, while the log-linear trend model assumes approximately constant proportional growth.

Chapter 07 demonstrated that these assumptions generate substantially different projections for 2030.

The linear specifications project CPU demand between approximately 1,263.85 and 1,369.90 million core-hours in 2030, depending on whether the 2026 observation is included.

By comparison, the log-linear specifications project approximately 2,479.16 to 2,502.07 million core-hours.

The considerably higher log-linear projections arise from the compounding of proportional growth over the forecast horizon.

Although the log-linear model performs slightly better on MAE, the historical evaluation does not provide sufficiently strong evidence that compounded growth will persist through 2030.

The linear trend is therefore more suitable as the leading candidate for a transparent central statistical projection, while the log-linear specification remains useful for examining alternative growth assumptions.

These projections are model-based trajectories rather than statistical prediction intervals or independently validated demand scenarios.


### CPU Model Selection Decision

The linear trend model fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series is selected as the central statistical forecasting specification for CPU demand.

This decision reflects the combined assessment of historical forecast accuracy, model interpretability, and the implications of extrapolating alternative growth assumptions through 2030.

The linear model achieved the lowest rolling-origin RMSE of 157.72 million core-hours, compared with 160.18 million core-hours for the log-linear model. However, the log-linear model achieved a slightly lower MAE of 133.74 million core-hours, compared with 138.65 million core-hours for the linear model.

These differences are relatively small and do not establish a statistically significant advantage for either model. Both specifications therefore remain plausible candidates based on their historical one-step-ahead forecasting performance.

The principal distinction lies in their growth assumptions. The linear model extrapolates an approximately constant absolute increase in CPU demand, whereas the log-linear model extrapolates compounded proportional growth.

Chapter 07 showed that these assumptions produce substantially different 2030 projections. The linear specifications generate approximately 1.26–1.37 billion core-hours, while the log-linear specifications generate approximately 2.48–2.50 billion core-hours.

Given the short annual series, differences in historical measurement and conversion assumptions, and uncertainty regarding the persistence of recent demand growth, the evidence is insufficient to establish compounded growth as the most appropriate central forecasting assumption.

The linear model is therefore preferred for the central statistical projection because it provides competitive historical forecast accuracy while retaining a simpler and more transparent growth assumption.

The following specifications are retained:

- **Central statistical projection:** Linear trend fitted to the complete 2014–2026 historical series.
- **Historical sensitivity comparison:** Linear trend fitted to 2014–2025, excluding the latest observation.
- **Alternative growth assumption:** Log-linear trend, retained to examine the implications of sustained proportional growth.

The 2026-excluded linear specification provides a sensitivity comparison rather than an independently validated lower bound. Similarly, the log-linear specification represents an alternative growth trajectory, not a statistically established upper bound.

The selected CPU model should be interpreted as a projection of the harmonised historical CPU demand measure, subject to the provenance, conversion, and reporting limitations documented in earlier chapters.

Chapter 10 will use these specifications to examine alternative CPU demand trajectories through 2030 without treating differences between models as formal prediction intervals.


## GPU Demand Model Selection

### Comparison of Candidate Models

GPU demand was evaluated separately from overall HPC and CPU demand because of its shorter historical coverage, differences in data provenance, and greater uncertainty surrounding the measurement of computational requirements.

The GPU forecasting models developed in Chapter 07 use five annual demand estimates covering 2022–2026. These estimates combine reported Setonix-based demand information with values derived using allocation shares and working conversion assumptions.

Four candidate models were assessed in Chapter 08: naïve forecasting, drift, linear trend, and log-linear trend.

An expanding-window, one-step-ahead rolling-origin evaluation was conducted using the available recent-period observations. Unlike the overall HPC and CPU evaluations, the GPU comparison contains only two test years, 2025 and 2026.

| Model | RMSE (million GPU-hours) | MAE (million GPU-hours) | RMSE Rank |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Naïve | 0.441 | 0.353 | 1 |
| Drift | 0.537 | 0.490 | 2 |
| Log-linear trend | 0.719 | 0.715 | 3 |
| Linear trend | 0.729 | 0.725 | 4 |

The naïve model achieved the lowest RMSE and MAE among the evaluated candidates. It therefore provided the strongest historical one-step-ahead predictive performance within this limited evaluation.

However, the comparison is based on only two forecast errors per model. Consequently, the resulting accuracy rankings may be strongly influenced by individual observations and should not be interpreted as robust evidence of long-term predictive superiority.

The naïve model assumes that future GPU demand remains at the most recently observed level. Although this assumption performed comparatively well during the evaluation period, it does not establish that GPU demand will remain constant through 2030.

Similarly, the higher forecast errors recorded by the linear and log-linear models do not establish that future GPU demand growth is absent. They indicate that these trend specifications were less accurate over the two available historical test years.

The results therefore support the naïve model as a short-term statistical benchmark, rather than a definitive long-term forecasting model.

### Sensitivity to the 2026 Observation

Chapter 07 examined the sensitivity of GPU trend estimates to the inclusion of the 2026 observation.

For the linear trend specification, the estimated annual increase changed from approximately 0.012 million GPU-hours when fitted to 2022–2025 to approximately 0.172 million GPU-hours when 2026 was included.

The log-linear trend coefficient also increased, from approximately 0.020 to 0.081.

These changes demonstrate that the estimated GPU growth trajectory is highly sensitive to the latest observation. However, the large relative percentage changes partly reflect the small trend coefficients estimated from the shorter 2022–2025 sample.

The resulting 2030 projections also differ substantially between specifications.

The linear trend models produce projected GPU demand ranging from approximately 2.252 to 3.371 million GPU-hours, while the log-linear models produce projections ranging from approximately 2.406 to 3.681 million GPU-hours.

These values represent alternative model-based trajectories, not formal prediction intervals or statistically validated lower and upper demand bounds.

The short modelling period and mixture of reported and estimated demand values limit confidence in extrapolating any single trend through 2030.

Furthermore, the historical GPU conversion scenarios developed in Chapter 05 represent uncertainty associated with conversion assumptions. They should not be interpreted as additional observed GPU demand data or as independent evidence of future growth.

Consequently, the GPU model selection process must distinguish between short-term predictive performance, sensitivity to individual observations, and the broader uncertainty surrounding future GPU requirements.


### GPU Model Selection Decision

The naïve forecasting model is retained as the preferred statistical benchmark for GPU demand because it achieved the lowest RMSE (0.441 million GPU-hours) and MAE (0.353 million GPU-hours) in the rolling-origin evaluation conducted in Chapter 08.

However, no single GPU forecasting model is selected as a sufficiently validated central projection through 2030.

This decision reflects three considerations.

First, the evaluation contains only two one-step-ahead test observations, covering 2025 and 2026. Although the naïve model performed best within this comparison, the limited number of forecast errors does not provide robust evidence that it will remain the most accurate model over a four-year forecasting horizon.

Second, the GPU modelling series contains only five annual estimates from 2022–2026. These estimates combine reported Setonix-based demand information with values derived using allocation shares and working conversion assumptions. Differences in data provenance limit the comparability of observations and the reliability of estimated growth trends.

Third, the linear and log-linear specifications are highly sensitive to the inclusion of 2026. Chapter 07 demonstrated substantial changes in their estimated trend coefficients and resulting 2030 projections when the latest observation was included or excluded.

The naïve model is therefore retained as a reference trajectory representing unchanged demand relative to the latest observation. It should not be interpreted as evidence that future GPU requirements will remain constant.

The linear and log-linear trend models are retained as exploratory growth trajectories. These specifications illustrate how projected demand changes under alternative assumptions about absolute and proportional growth, but their historical performance and sensitivity do not justify selecting either as a definitive long-term forecast.

The following approach is adopted for subsequent scenario analysis:

- **Statistical reference benchmark:** Naïve model, representing unchanged GPU demand from the latest observation.
- **Exploratory absolute-growth trajectory:** Linear trend fitted to the available 2022–2026 estimates.
- **Exploratory proportional-growth trajectory:** Log-linear trend fitted to the available 2022–2026 estimates.
- **Historical sensitivity comparisons:** Alternative trend specifications excluding 2026, used to assess dependence on the latest observation.
- **Conversion uncertainty:** Historical GPU conversion scenarios retained separately from future growth assumptions.

These trajectories are not assigned probabilities or interpreted as statistically validated lower, central, and upper demand bounds.

Consequently, the GPU analysis provides an exploratory assessment of possible future resource requirements rather than a definitive forecast of national research GPU demand.

Chapter 10 will present these alternatives transparently, distinguishing historical measurement uncertainty from assumptions about future demand growth.


## Field of Research Scenario Assumptions

### Purpose and Analytical Approach

Field of Research (FoR) is incorporated into the forecasting framework to examine how projected national HPC demand could be distributed across research disciplines under alternative allocation-composition assumptions.

Unlike overall HPC, CPU, and GPU demand, the harmonised FoR dataset primarily represents historically allocated resources rather than directly observed requests for computational resources within individual research fields.

Consequently, FoR-specific demand cannot be independently forecast and validated using the same approach applied to the aggregate resource series.

Instead, a top-down scenario framework is adopted. Under this approach, projected overall HPC demand is distributed across harmonised FoR categories using explicit assumptions about future allocation shares.

These estimates represent hypothetical distributions of projected demand based on historical allocation patterns, rather than independently validated forecasts of researcher demand within each field.

### Historical Evidence for Scenario Assumptions

Chapter 08 evaluated harmonised FoR allocation shares across 2014–2026, including separate assessments of the earlier 2014–2020 and recent 2021–2026 periods.

During 2021–2026, Engineering & Technology accounted for the largest average allocation share (34.88%), followed by Physical Sciences (22.13%), Chemical Sciences (17.84%), and Earth & Environmental Sciences (14.00%).

The longer historical comparison identified substantial differences in recorded allocation composition between reporting periods.

Engineering & Technology increased from an average allocation share of 16.90% in 2014–2020 to 34.88% in 2021–2026, representing a difference of +17.98 percentage points.

Conversely, Earth & Environmental Sciences decreased from 31.62% to 14.00%, a difference of -17.61 percentage points.

Physical Sciences increased by 5.21 percentage points, while Mathematics & Computing decreased by 2.46 percentage points.

These changes demonstrate that the recorded distribution of HPC allocations has varied over time. However, they cannot be interpreted exclusively as changes in underlying researcher demand because the historical series spans different FoR classification standards, reporting coverage, and allocation practices.

The observed differences therefore provide evidence for exploring alternative allocation-composition assumptions, but not for extrapolating discipline-specific demand growth rates directly.

### Selection of FoR Scenario Assumptions

Three allocation-composition approaches are proposed for scenario development in Chapter 10.

**Recent-period composition**

The first approach uses the harmonised allocation-share patterns observed during 2021–2026 as a reference for a contemporary allocation-composition scenario.

This approach reflects the more recent reporting framework and provides a transparent historical basis for distributing projected overall HPC demand across research fields.

However, the observed annual shares are not constant, and some categories are missing from particular years. The implementation must therefore define how category coverage, missing observations, and share normalisation are handled.

**Latest-year composition**

The second approach uses the 2026 allocation-share distribution as an alternative reference.

This approach illustrates the implications of maintaining the latest observed composition throughout the projection horizon.

However, a single year's allocation shares may reflect temporary conditions or reporting-specific effects. The 2026 composition should therefore not automatically be considered more representative of future researcher demand than the recent-period average.

**Historical-composition sensitivity**

The third approach uses the earlier 2014–2020 allocation composition to examine how projected demand distributions change under a different historical allocation pattern.

This approach is intended as a sensitivity comparison rather than a direct prediction that earlier allocation patterns will return.

Differences in FoR classification frameworks, reporting scope, and category coverage must be considered before constructing comparable scenario shares.

### Interpretation and Limitations

The proposed FoR scenarios are conditional on two distinct assumptions:

1. The selected overall HPC model provides an indicative trajectory of total national research HPC demand.
2. Historical FoR allocation shares provide a reasonable reference for constructing hypothetical distributions of that projected demand.

Neither assumption establishes that the resulting FoR-level estimates represent directly observed or independently validated future researcher demand.

The allocation shares must also form a complete and internally consistent distribution for each scenario. Missing categories should not automatically be treated as zero allocations, and any normalisation or category exclusions must be documented.

Furthermore, changes in allocation composition may reflect resource constraints, institutional priorities, facility coverage, or reporting conventions rather than changes in unconstrained computational requirements.

For these reasons, FoR scenarios will be presented as allocation-share-based distributions of projected overall demand, not as independent field-specific demand forecasts.

Chapter 10 will implement these assumptions and examine how alternative historical compositions affect the indicative distribution of HPC requirements through 2030.

The selected approaches reflect differences in data availability, predictive evidence, and the interpretation of each demand measure.

For overall HPC, the linear trend provides the strongest historical one-step-ahead forecasting performance among the evaluated models. Its full-history specification is selected as the central statistical projection, while the 2026-excluded specification is retained for sensitivity analysis.

For CPU demand, the linear trend is also selected as the central statistical projection. Although the log-linear model achieved a slightly lower MAE, the linear model achieved the lowest RMSE and avoids assuming that historical proportional growth will necessarily continue through 2030.

For GPU demand, the naïve model is retained as the preferred statistical benchmark. ### Forecasting Uncertainty and Data Limitations

Several limitations apply across the selected forecasting approaches.

**Historical measurement and comparability**

The harmonised historical series combine directly reported observations, derived estimates, and proxy-based values. Differences in resource accounting, reporting coverage, and conversion assumptions may affect comparability across years.

Consequently, the resulting forecasts should be interpreted as projections of the constructed historical demand measures rather than definitive estimates of unconstrained national researcher requirements.

**Limited annual observations**

The overall HPC and CPU forecasting series contain 13 annual observations covering 2014–2026.

Although rolling-origin cross-validation provides useful out-of-sample evidence, the six available evaluation years limit the precision with which differences in predictive performance can be established.

The GPU series contains only five recent annual estimates and two evaluation years, resulting in substantially greater model-selection uncertainty.

**Sensitivity to recent demand changes**

The substantial increase in recorded overall HPC demand in 2026 affects estimated historical trends and projected future demand.

The influence of this observation is examined using alternative specifications that exclude 2026. These comparisons assess sensitivity but do not establish whether the observed increase represents a temporary change, a sustained acceleration, or differences in reporting and measurement.

**Long-horizon extrapolation**

The rolling-origin evaluations primarily assess one-step-ahead predictive performance.

Forecasts extending to 2030 require additional assumptions about the persistence of historical trends. Strong performance over a one-year horizon does not guarantee equivalent performance over four years.

**Resource-specific interpretation**

Overall HPC demand is expressed in kSU, CPU demand in core-hours, and GPU demand in GPU-hours.

These measures describe different aspects of computing requirements and should not be directly added or compared without an explicitly justified conversion framework.

**FoR allocation-share interpretation**

FoR allocation shares represent the recorded distribution of allocated resources rather than directly observed field-specific demand.

The resulting scenarios depend on assumptions about how historical allocation composition may relate to the future distribution of computational requirements. They are not independently validated FoR demand forecasts.

### Implications for Scenario Development

The model-selection findings establish the analytical basis for Chapter 10.

The full-history linear trend models will provide central statistical projections for overall HPC and CPU demand, with 2026-excluded specifications used to examine sensitivity to the latest observation.

Alternative log-linear trajectories will illustrate the implications of compounded proportional growth. These alternatives will not automatically be interpreted as statistically established upper demand bounds.

For GPU demand, the naïve benchmark and exploratory trend specifications will be presented separately, reflecting the limited evidence available for selecting a definitive long-term forecasting model.

For FoR, three historical allocation-share reference periods will be considered: earlier (2014 - 2020), recent (2021 - 2026), and long-term (2014 - 2026). The resulting normalised allocation-share weights will be applied to the central overall HPC demand projection to examine how the illustrative distribution across research fields varies with the selected reference period. These distributions are conditional planning scenarios rather than independently estimated FoR demand forecasts.

Scenario development will distinguish three sources of uncertainty:

1. **Historical measurement uncertainty:** Differences in data provenance, reporting scope, and conversion assumptions.
2. **Model uncertainty:** Differences between forecasting specifications and their underlying growth assumptions.
3. **Scenario uncertainty:** Alternative assumptions about future demand growth and the distribution of computational requirements.

These sources of uncertainty will not be treated as interchangeable.

The scenarios will therefore provide transparent, assumption-dependent projections for national HPC infrastructure planning rather than probabilistic estimates of all possible future outcomes.
