7.1 Purpose

The purpose of this stage is to examine historical demand and allocation patterns for Australian national High-Performance Computing (HPC) resources from 2014 to 2026, using the converted datasets produced in Chapter 05.

The analysis focuses on identifying trends in computational resource demand, allocation levels, and potential differences between requested and allocated resources. CPU core-hours and GPU-hours are examined separately because they represent distinct computational resource types and cannot be directly combined.

The analysis also examines the distribution of HPC allocations across Fields of Research (FoR) to identify how allocated computational resources are distributed among research disciplines and how these patterns have changed over time.

Particular attention is given to historical reporting differences, conversion assumptions, and estimated resource values. These limitations are considered when interpreting trends and assessing the suitability of the data for forecasting.

The findings from this stage will inform subsequent forecasting model development, evaluation, and scenario analysis through 2030.

7.2 Historical Demand and Allocation

Historical HPC demand and allocation are examined across the 2014 - 2026 analytical period to identify changes in requested computational resources, awarded allocations, and the difference between them.

Requested compute is used as an indicator of researcher demand, while allocated compute reflects the resources awarded within available infrastructure and programme constraints. These measures are therefore interpreted separately.

The analysis uses the converted annual kSU series to examine demand and allocation trends, annual growth rates, request allocation gaps, and fulfilment rates. However, historical values include directly reported observations, derived estimates, and capacity-based proxies. Consequently, observed changes may partly reflect reporting differences or changes in infrastructure rather than underlying demand alone.

7.2.4 Initial Findings

The annual HPC demand and allocation series show an overall upward trend between 2014 and 2026. Reported or estimated demand increases from approximately 131,356 kSU in 2014 to 2,242,817 kSU in 2026, while allocation increases from approximately 83,505 kSU to 872,288 kSU.

Demand exceeds allocation in every analytical year. The largest absolute gap occurs in 2026, when demand exceeds allocation by approximately 1.37 million kSU. The corresponding fulfilment rate is approximately 38.9%, compared with 56.0% in 2025.

The series also exhibits periods of substantial change, particularly around 2018, 2021, and 2026. However, these changes should not automatically be interpreted as equivalent increases in underlying researcher demand. Historical conversion assumptions, differences in reporting scope, capacity-based allocation proxies, and changes in HPC systems may contribute to the observed variation.

These findings suggest a persistent difference between requested and allocated computational resources. Further analysis of CPU and GPU resources is required to understand whether similar patterns are present across distinct computational resource types.

7.3 CPU and GPU Resources

CPU and GPU resources are examined separately to identify historical patterns in computational demand and allocation across the 2014–2026 analytical period.

The analysis uses the converted CPU core-hours and GPU-hours produced in Chapter 05. These measures provide resource-specific perspectives on HPC demand and allocation, although their interpretation remains subject to system-level accounting conventions and historical conversion assumptions.

Historical CPU estimates are influenced by the assumed resource composition and conversion factors applied to earlier observations. GPU estimates for 2014 - 2021 are based on low, baseline, and high scenarios rather than directly observed resource-level demand and allocation. GPU values for 2022 - 2026 use reported system-level allocations and a combination of reported and estimated demand.

CPU core-hours and GPU-hours are therefore analysed independently and are not added together. The objective is to examine trends within each resource category while retaining the distinction between reported observations and modelled estimates.

7.3.3 Initial Findings

The resource-specific analysis indicates an overall increase in estimated CPU and GPU demand between 2014 and 2026, although the two series exhibit different patterns and levels of uncertainty.

CPU Resources

Estimated CPU demand increases from approximately 130.0 million CPU core-hours in 2014 to 1,235.0 million CPU core-hours in 2026. Over the same period, CPU allocation increases from approximately 82.7 million to 518.6 million CPU core-hours.

CPU demand remains above allocation throughout the analytical period. Substantial fluctuations are visible around 2018 – 2022, followed by a sustained increase in estimated demand from 2022 to 2026. In contrast, CPU allocation remains relatively stable between 2023 and 2026, at approximately 491 – 528 million CPU core-hours.

The widening difference between CPU demand and allocation in recent years suggests increasing pressure on allocated CPU resources. However, the magnitude of historical changes should be interpreted cautiously because earlier observations include assumed conversion factors, capacity proxies, and estimated CPU/GPU resource shares.

GPU Resources

Estimated GPU demand increases from approximately 0.04 million GPU-hours in 2014 to 3.00 million GPU-hours in 2026. GPU allocation increases from approximately 0.02 million to 1.25 million GPU-hours over the same period.

The GPU series shows a pronounced increase between 2021 and 2022, followed by fluctuations in estimated demand and comparatively stable allocations during 2022–2026. By 2026, estimated GPU demand is approximately 2.4 times the allocated GPU-hours.

However, the historical GPU series requires particular caution. Values for 2014–2021 are based on assumed GPU resource shares and a provisional conversion factor rather than directly observed GPU demand and allocation. The low, baseline, and high scenarios demonstrate that historical GPU estimates are sensitive to these assumptions.

Furthermore, GPU allocation from 2022 onward is derived from reported system-level service units using a working conversion factor, while GPU demand includes both directly reported and estimated values. The apparent increase around 2022 may therefore partly reflect changes in reporting coverage, infrastructure, and conversion methodology rather than an equivalent increase in underlying GPU demand.

Implications for Forecasting

The CPU and GPU series suggest different historical demand patterns and should be considered separately during forecasting model development. The observed differences between demand and allocation also reinforce the importance of distinguishing researcher demand from computational resources awarded under capacity constraints.

Historical uncertainty, system transitions, and the limited availability of directly observed GPU demand should be considered when selecting modelling periods and evaluating forecast reliability. Scenario analysis will be particularly important for assessing the sensitivity of GPU forecasts to historical conversion assumptions.

7.4 Field of Research

The Field of Research (FoR) analysis examines the distribution of national HPC allocations across research disciplines during the 2014 – 2026 analytical period.

The analysis uses the standardised research-field classifications and converted allocation measures produced in Chapters 04 and 05. It focuses on identifying the largest research-field allocation shares, changes in disciplinary composition over time, and differences in allocated CPU and GPU resources.

FoR-level CPU core-hours and GPU-hours are estimated using each field’s share of annual HPC allocations. Consequently, these resource-specific estimates reflect proportional allocation assumptions rather than directly observed CPU or GPU usage by individual research disciplines.

The 2017 observation is interpreted cautiously because its FoR reporting scope differs from the NCMAS-based observations used in other years.

The objective is to identify historical allocation patterns that may inform subsequent forecasting, without interpreting allocation shares as direct measures of researcher demand.

# Initial Analysis

## Purpose

The purpose of this stage is to examine historical demand and allocation patterns for Australian national High-Performance Computing (HPC) resources from 2014 to 2026, using the converted datasets produced in Chapter 05.

The analysis focuses on identifying trends in computational resource demand, allocation levels, and potential differences between requested and allocated resources. CPU core-hours and GPU-hours are examined separately because they represent distinct computational resource types and cannot be directly combined.

The analysis also examines the distribution of HPC allocations across Fields of Research (FoR) to identify how allocated computational resources are distributed among research disciplines and how these patterns have changed over time.

Particular attention is given to historical reporting differences, conversion assumptions, and estimated resource values. These limitations are considered when interpreting trends and assessing the suitability of the data for forecasting.

The findings from this stage will inform subsequent forecasting model development, evaluation, and scenario analysis through 2030.

## Historical Demand and Allocation

Historical HPC demand and allocation are examined across the 2014 - 2026 analytical period to identify changes in requested computational resources, awarded allocations, and the difference between them.

Requested compute is used as an indicator of researcher demand, while allocated compute reflects the resources awarded within available infrastructure and programme constraints. These measures are therefore interpreted separately.

The analysis uses the converted annual kSU series to examine demand and allocation trends, annual growth rates, request allocation gaps, and fulfilment rates. However, historical values include directly reported observations, derived estimates, and capacity-based proxies. Consequently, observed changes may partly reflect reporting differences or changes in infrastructure rather than underlying demand alone.

### Initial Findings

The annual HPC demand and allocation series show an overall upward trend between 2014 and 2026. Reported or estimated demand increases from approximately 131,356 kSU in 2014 to 2,242,817 kSU in 2026, while allocation increases from approximately 83,505 kSU to 872,288 kSU.

Demand exceeds allocation in every analytical year. The largest absolute gap occurs in 2026, when demand exceeds allocation by approximately 1.37 million kSU. The corresponding fulfilment rate is approximately 38.9%, compared with 56.0% in 2025.

The series also exhibits periods of substantial change, particularly around 2018, 2021, and 2026. However, these changes should not automatically be interpreted as equivalent increases in underlying researcher demand. Historical conversion assumptions, differences in reporting scope, capacity-based allocation proxies, and changes in HPC systems may contribute to the observed variation.

These findings suggest a persistent difference between requested and allocated computational resources. Further analysis of CPU and GPU resources is required to understand whether similar patterns are present across distinct computational resource types.

## CPU and GPU Resources

CPU and GPU resources are examined separately to identify historical patterns in computational demand and allocation across the 2014–2026 analytical period.

The analysis uses the converted CPU core-hours and GPU-hours produced in Chapter 05. These measures provide resource-specific perspectives on HPC demand and allocation, although their interpretation remains subject to system-level accounting conventions and historical conversion assumptions.

Historical CPU estimates are influenced by the assumed resource composition and conversion factors applied to earlier observations. GPU estimates for 2014 - 2021 are based on low, baseline, and high scenarios rather than directly observed resource-level demand and allocation. GPU values for 2022 - 2026 use reported system-level allocations and a combination of reported and estimated demand.

CPU core-hours and GPU-hours are therefore analysed independently and are not added together. The objective is to examine trends within each resource category while retaining the distinction between reported observations and modelled estimates.

### Initial Findings

The resource-specific analysis indicates an overall increase in estimated CPU and GPU demand between 2014 and 2026, although the two series exhibit different patterns and levels of uncertainty.

**CPU Resources**

Estimated CPU demand increases from approximately 130.0 million CPU core-hours in 2014 to 1,235.0 million CPU core-hours in 2026. Over the same period, CPU allocation increases from approximately 82.7 million to 518.6 million CPU core-hours.

CPU demand remains above allocation throughout the analytical period. Substantial fluctuations are visible around 2018 – 2022, followed by a sustained increase in estimated demand from 2022 to 2026. In contrast, CPU allocation remains relatively stable between 2023 and 2026, at approximately 491 – 528 million CPU core-hours.

The widening difference between CPU demand and allocation in recent years suggests increasing pressure on allocated CPU resources. However, the magnitude of historical changes should be interpreted cautiously because earlier observations include assumed conversion factors, capacity proxies, and estimated CPU/GPU resource shares.

**GPU Resources**

Estimated GPU demand increases from approximately 0.04 million GPU-hours in 2014 to 3.00 million GPU-hours in 2026. GPU allocation increases from approximately 0.02 million to 1.25 million GPU-hours over the same period.

The GPU series shows a pronounced increase between 2021 and 2022, followed by fluctuations in estimated demand and comparatively stable allocations during 2022–2026. By 2026, estimated GPU demand is approximately 2.4 times the allocated GPU-hours.

However, the historical GPU series requires particular caution. Values for 2014–2021 are based on assumed GPU resource shares and a provisional conversion factor rather than directly observed GPU demand and allocation. The low, baseline, and high scenarios demonstrate that historical GPU estimates are sensitive to these assumptions.

Furthermore, GPU allocation from 2022 onward is derived from reported system-level service units using a working conversion factor, while GPU demand includes both directly reported and estimated values. The apparent increase around 2022 may therefore partly reflect changes in reporting coverage, infrastructure, and conversion methodology rather than an equivalent increase in underlying GPU demand.

**Implications for Forecasting**

The CPU and GPU series suggest different historical demand patterns and should be considered separately during forecasting model development. The observed differences between demand and allocation also reinforce the importance of distinguishing researcher demand from computational resources awarded under capacity constraints.

Historical uncertainty, system transitions, and the limited availability of directly observed GPU demand should be considered when selecting modelling periods and evaluating forecast reliability. Scenario analysis will be particularly important for assessing the sensitivity of GPU forecasts to historical conversion assumptions.

## Field of Research

The Field of Research (FoR) analysis examines the distribution of national HPC allocations across research disciplines during the 2014 – 2026 analytical period.

The analysis uses the standardised research-field classifications and converted allocation measures produced in Chapters 04 and 05. It focuses on identifying the largest research-field allocation shares, changes in disciplinary composition over time, and differences in allocated CPU and GPU resources.

FoR-level CPU core-hours and GPU-hours are estimated using each field's share of annual HPC allocations. Consequently, these resource-specific estimates reflect proportional allocation assumptions rather than directly observed CPU or GPU usage by individual research disciplines.

The 2017 observation is interpreted cautiously because its FoR reporting scope differs from the NCMAS-based observations used in other years.

The objective is to identify historical allocation patterns that may inform subsequent forecasting, without interpreting allocation shares as direct measures of researcher demand.

For longitudinal comparison, the original FoR classifications are consolidated into eight broader analytical groups. These groupings reduce inconsistencies arising from differences in naming conventions and classification structures across reporting years. They are intended for exploratory visualisation and do not represent official ANZSRC categories. Records without a reliable disciplinary classification, including operational allocations and categories without a direct FoR alignment, are retained separately as Unclassified & Operational.
