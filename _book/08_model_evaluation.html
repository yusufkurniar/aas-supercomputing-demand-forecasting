9.1 Purpose

This chapter evaluates the predictive performance and statistical suitability of the candidate forecasting models developed in Chapter 07.

The evaluation considers three distinct measures of Australian national research supercomputing demand:

  1. Overall HPC demand, expressed in thousands of service units (kSU).
  2. CPU demand, expressed in millions of CPU core-hours.
  3. GPU demand, expressed in millions of GPU-hours.

The objective is to identify forecasting approaches that provide a defensible basis for estimating researcher demand through 2030, while recognising limitations in historical data quality, comparability, and sample size.

Model selection will prioritise predictive accuracy, simplicity, interpretability, and robustness to influential observations, particularly the increase in reported demand in 2026.

9.2 Evaluation Framework

9.2.1 Candidate Models and Benchmarks

The evaluation considers four forecasting approaches:

  1. Naïve benchmark: forecasts future demand using the most recent available observation.
  2. Drift benchmark: extrapolates the average annual change between the first and most recent observations in the training sample.
  3. Linear trend: estimates a constant annual absolute change in demand.
  4. Log-linear trend: estimates approximately constant proportional growth using a regression model fitted to log-transformed demand.

Naïve and drift forecasts provide simple reference points against which the regression-based models can be evaluated.

The linear and log-linear specifications follow the modelling framework established in Chapter 07, including sensitivity analyses excluding the 2026 observation.

More complex models will not be prioritised unless their additional complexity can be justified by the available data and demonstrated predictive performance.

9.2.2 Time-Series Cross-Validation

Forecast accuracy will be assessed using rolling-origin time-series cross-validation.

At each evaluation origin, models will be estimated using only observations available up to that year. Forecasts will then be compared with subsequent historical observations.

This approach preserves chronological ordering and avoids using future observations to estimate models evaluated on earlier periods.

One-step-ahead evaluation will provide the primary comparison because the historical series are relatively short.

Where sufficient observations are available, additional forecast horizons may be examined to assess the stability of longer-term extrapolations.

The evaluation will distinguish between the longer overall HPC and CPU series covering 2014–2026 and the shorter GPU modelling series covering 2022 - 2026.

For GPU demand, the small number of recent observations severely limits the number of meaningful cross-validation origins. Any accuracy comparisons will therefore be considered exploratory.

9.2.3 Forecast Accuracy Measures

Forecast errors will be evaluated on the original scale of each demand measure using:

  1. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), which gives greater weight to larger forecast errors.
  2. Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which measures the average absolute forecast error.

Accuracy measures will be calculated separately for overall HPC, CPU, and GPU demand because their units are different.

For log-linear models, predictions will be back-transformed to the original demand scale before calculating forecast errors. The evaluation will distinguish exponentiated log-scale predictions from bias-adjusted mean forecasts where relevant.

Models will be compared using common evaluation origins within each demand measure to ensure a fair comparison.

9.2.4 Model Selection Principles

The preferred forecasting approach will not be selected solely on the basis of the lowest numerical error.

Model selection will also consider:

  • Stability across evaluation periods and forecast origins.
  • Sensitivity to the inclusion of the 2026 observation.
  • Residual behaviour and evidence of systematic model errors.
  • Interpretability and consistency with historical data provenance.
  • The risks of extrapolating growth patterns through 2030.

Particular caution will be applied to GPU demand because its historical modelling period is short and includes estimates derived from different reporting and conversion methods.

The evaluation results presented in this chapter will provide the statistical evidence for model selection in Chapter 09 and the development of demand scenarios in Chapter 10.

9.3 Rolling-Origin Cross-Validation

9.3.1 Overall HPC Demand: One-Step-Ahead Evaluation

The initial evaluation focuses on overall HPC demand, measured in thousands of service units (kSU).

Rolling-origin cross-validation is applied using an initial training period of 2014 - 2020. At each subsequent origin, the training sample expands by one year, and a one-step-ahead forecast is generated for the following year.

This produces six evaluation years, from 2021 to 2026.

Four forecasting approaches are evaluated using identical training periods and forecast origins:

  1. Naïve benchmark.
  2. Drift benchmark.
  3. Linear trend.
  4. Log-linear trend.

Each model is re-estimated using only the observations available at the corresponding forecast origin. This avoids information leakage from future observations.

Code 
# Load harmonised annual demand data
annual_converted <- readRDS(
  "assets/data/data converted/annual_demand_allocation_converted.rds"
)

# Prepare overall HPC demand series
overall_cv_data <- annual_converted |>
  dplyr::transmute(
    year = as.integer(year),
    demand_ksu = as.numeric(demand_ksu)
  ) |>
  dplyr::arrange(year)

# Validate historical series
stopifnot(
  identical(overall_cv_data$year, 2014:2026),
  all(is.finite(overall_cv_data$demand_ksu)),
  all(overall_cv_data$demand_ksu > 0)
)

# Define common one-step-ahead evaluation years
cv_test_years <- 2021:2026

# Generate forecasts using expanding training windows
overall_cv_predictions <- dplyr::bind_rows(
  lapply(cv_test_years, function(test_year) {

    training <- overall_cv_data |>
      dplyr::filter(year < test_year) |>
      dplyr::mutate(time = year - 2014)

    actual <- overall_cv_data$demand_ksu[
      overall_cv_data$year == test_year
    ]

    future <- data.frame(time = test_year - 2014)

    # Naïve: last observed demand
    naive_forecast <- tail(training$demand_ksu, 1)

    # Drift: average change from first to last observation
    drift_forecast <- tail(training$demand_ksu, 1) +
      (
        tail(training$demand_ksu, 1) -
          training$demand_ksu[1]
      ) / (nrow(training) - 1)

    # Linear trend fitted only to training data
    linear_fit <- lm(
      demand_ksu ~ time,
      data = training
    )

    linear_forecast <- as.numeric(
      predict(linear_fit, newdata = future)
    )

    # Log-linear trend fitted only to training data
    loglinear_fit <- lm(
      log(demand_ksu) ~ time,
      data = training
    )

    loglinear_forecast <- exp(as.numeric(
      predict(loglinear_fit, newdata = future)
    ))

    data.frame(
      year = test_year,
      actual = actual,
      naive = naive_forecast,
      drift = drift_forecast,
      linear = linear_forecast,
      loglinear = loglinear_forecast
    )
  })
)

Forecast accuracy is evaluated using root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) across the six one-step-ahead predictions for 2021 - 2026.

Both measures are calculated on the original kSU scale. Lower values indicate better historical predictive accuracy over the common evaluation period.

9.3.3 Overall HPC Accuracy Findings

Rolling-origin one-step-ahead cross-validation over 2021–2026 indicates that the linear trend model achieves the lowest forecast errors among the four evaluated approaches.

The linear model records an RMSE of approximately 290,898 kSU and an MAE of 198,295 kSU. The drift benchmark ranks second, with an RMSE of 314,588 kSU and an MAE of 221,778 kSU.

The naïve benchmark produces an RMSE of 378,864 kSU, while the log-linear model records the highest RMSE of 511,614 kSU and MAE of 466,991 kSU.

These findings indicate that the linear trend provides the strongest one-step-ahead predictive performance over the selected evaluation period.

Although the log-linear model exhibited a relatively high in-sample R-squared value in Chapter 07, its weaker cross-validation accuracy demonstrates that in-sample fit alone is insufficient for selecting a forecasting model.

The evaluation is nevertheless based on only six forecast errors and includes the substantial demand increase in 2026. Consequently, the model rankings may be sensitive to the selected evaluation period and individual observations.

Furthermore, one-step-ahead accuracy does not necessarily translate into superior multi-year forecasting performance through 2030.

9.4 Field of Research Allocation-Share Evaluation

9.4.1 Purpose and Evaluation Approach

The Field of Research (FoR) evaluation examines the historical distribution and stability of HPC resource allocations across research fields.

Unlike the overall HPC, CPU, and GPU evaluations, this analysis does not assess independently observed FoR-specific researcher demand forecasts.

Instead, it evaluates whether historical allocation shares provide a sufficiently consistent basis for developing indicative FoR demand scenarios through 2030.

The analysis focuses on 2021–2026, corresponding to the FoR 2020 classification period used in Chapter 06.

Three considerations guide the evaluation:

  1. Historical stability of FoR allocation shares.
  2. Variation between the latest-year composition and the recent-period average.
  3. Sensitivity to classification, reporting coverage, and allocation-based measurement assumptions.

The results will inform the selection of FoR share assumptions in Chapter 09 and their application to overall HPC demand forecasts in Chapter 10.

9.4.2 Harmonised FoR Data Preparation

The FoR evaluation reuses the harmonised allocation-share datasets developed in Chapter 06, ensuring consistency between historical analysis and model evaluation.

Two reporting periods are considered:

  • The full historical period (2014 - 2026), using broad harmonised analytical groups to examine long-term allocation composition.
  • The recent period (2021–2026), using FoR 2020-based analytical groups to evaluate recent allocation-share stability.

The full-period analysis provides historical context, while the recent-period analysis informs the assumptions used for indicative FoR demand scenarios.

9.4.7 FoR Evaluation Findings and Limitations

The Field of Research (FoR) evaluation provides evidence on the historical composition and variability of HPC resource allocations across research fields. Unlike the overall HPC, CPU, and GPU evaluations, which assess forecasting performance against historical resource measures, the FoR evaluation is descriptive. Its purpose is to assess the stability and comparability of allocation shares rather than to establish predictive accuracy for research-field demand.

Historical allocation-share patterns

The 2021 - 2026 evaluation shows that Engineering & Technology accounted for the largest average allocation share (34.88%), followed by Physical Sciences (22.13%), Chemical Sciences (17.84%), and Earth & Environmental Sciences (14.00%). These categories were observed in all six years, although their shares varied over time.

The longer 2014 - 2026 evaluation indicates that allocation composition was not constant across the historical period. Engineering & Technology and Earth & Environmental Sciences each exhibited a range of 29.65 percentage points, while Physical Sciences exhibited a range of 26.63 percentage points. Chemical Sciences was comparatively stable, with a range of 4.69 percentage points.

The separate 2014 - 2020 evaluation provides additional context. Earth & Environmental Sciences accounted for the largest average allocation share (31.62%), followed by Chemical Sciences (19.02%), Physical Sciences (16.92%), and Engineering & Technology (16.90%). Several categories exhibited substantial variation during this earlier period.

Differences between reporting periods

Comparing the two periods using harmonised analytical categories reveals substantial differences in average allocation composition. Engineering & Technology increased from 16.90% in 2014 - 2020 to 34.88% in 2021 - 2026, a difference of +17.98 percentage points. Conversely, Earth & Environmental Sciences decreased from 31.62% to 14.00%, a difference of -17.61 percentage points. Physical Sciences recorded a higher average share (+5.21 percentage points), while Mathematics & Computing recorded a lower average (-2.46 percentage points).

These differences indicate changes in the recorded distribution of HPC allocations across research fields. They do not, however, establish equivalent changes in underlying researcher demand.

Data and interpretation limitations

Several limitations affect the interpretation of these findings:

  1. Allocation versus demand: The FoR dataset represents allocated HPC resources, not directly observed research-field requests or unconstrained computational needs. Allocation shares may therefore reflect resource availability, allocation decisions, and reporting practices as well as researcher activity.

  2. Classification comparability: The historical analysis spans FoR 2008 and FoR 2020 reporting frameworks. Although the categories were harmonised for analytical purposes in Chapter 06, they are not necessarily equivalent to official classifications across all years.

  3. Incomplete category coverage: Not all research-field groups are observed in every year. Missing observations were not automatically interpreted as zero allocations. Consequently, averages and ranges for categories with limited coverage are less informative than those for consistently reported categories.

  4. Reporting scope: Historical differences in facility coverage, resource accounting, and reporting conventions may influence the recorded allocation shares. Apparent changes in research-field composition cannot be attributed exclusively to changes in research priorities.

  5. Period-average interpretation: The comparison uses unweighted averages of available annual allocation shares. These describe the typical recorded composition within each period but do not represent shares of the total HPC resources allocated across all years combined.

  6. Limited predictive validation: The available FoR allocation-share series does not provide a consistent, directly observed research-field demand target suitable for evaluating field-specific demand forecasts using the same rolling-origin procedure applied to the overall HPC, CPU, and GPU series.

Implications for model selection and scenarios

The FoR evaluation supports the use of historical allocation composition as a reference for developing research-field scenarios, but not as a direct forecast of field-specific researcher demand.

The more recent 2021 - 2026 shares provide a relevant reference for a baseline allocation-composition assumption, subject to their observed variability and reporting limitations. The earlier period and the full historical series provide additional context for examining alternative compositions and assessing sensitivity to changes in research-field allocation patterns.

Accordingly, Chapter 09 will distinguish models selected through quantitative forecast-error evaluation from FoR assumptions supported by descriptive historical evidence. Any subsequent application of FoR shares to projected overall HPC demand will be presented as a scenario-based allocation of projected demand, rather than an independently validated forecast of demand within each research field.

9.5 Overall Evaluation Conclusions and Forecast-Horizon Limitations

The rolling-origin evaluation provides a consistent basis for comparing candidate forecasting models using historical one-step-ahead prediction errors. For overall HPC and CPU demand, six forecast errors are available for 2021–2026. For GPU demand, only two forecast errors are available for 2025–2026.

The results support the selection of a linear trend as the central forecasting approach for overall HPC and CPU demand. However, the evaluation does not directly establish the accuracy of forecasts two, three, or four years ahead. The relative performance of alternative models may change over longer horizons.

The GPU evaluation is particularly limited by its short historical series. Although the naïve benchmark performs best in the available one-step-ahead comparison, this does not establish that GPU demand will remain constant through 2030.

The FoR analysis evaluates historical allocation-share composition and variability rather than independently testing future demand forecasts by research field.

Consequently, the 2027–2030 projections should be interpreted as planning scenarios informed by historical predictive performance, not as forecasts with independently validated four-year-ahead accuracy. The alternative growth specifications, 2026-exclusion sensitivity analyses, and conditional prediction intervals in Chapter 10 provide additional perspectives on uncertainty but do not replace direct multi-horizon validation.

# Model Evaluation

## Purpose

This chapter evaluates the predictive performance and statistical suitability of the candidate forecasting models developed in Chapter 07.

The evaluation considers three distinct measures of Australian national research supercomputing demand:

1. Overall HPC demand, expressed in thousands of service units (kSU).
2. CPU demand, expressed in millions of CPU core-hours.
3. GPU demand, expressed in millions of GPU-hours.

The objective is to identify forecasting approaches that provide a defensible basis for estimating researcher demand through 2030,
while recognising limitations in historical data quality, comparability, and sample size.

Model selection will prioritise predictive accuracy, simplicity, interpretability, and robustness to influential observations, particularly the increase in reported demand in 2026.

## Evaluation Framework

### Candidate Models and Benchmarks

The evaluation considers four forecasting approaches:

1. Naïve benchmark: forecasts future demand using the most recent available observation.
2. Drift benchmark: extrapolates the average annual change between the first and most recent observations in the training sample.
3. Linear trend: estimates a constant annual absolute change in demand.
4. Log-linear trend: estimates approximately constant proportional growth using a regression model fitted to log-transformed demand.

Naïve and drift forecasts provide simple reference points against which the regression-based models can be evaluated.

The linear and log-linear specifications follow the modelling framework established in Chapter 07, including sensitivity analyses excluding the 2026 observation.

More complex models will not be prioritised unless their additional complexity can be justified by the available data and demonstrated predictive performance.

### Time-Series Cross-Validation

Forecast accuracy will be assessed using rolling-origin time-series cross-validation.

At each evaluation origin, models will be estimated using only observations available up to that year. Forecasts will then be compared with subsequent historical observations.

This approach preserves chronological ordering and avoids using future observations to estimate models evaluated on earlier periods.

One-step-ahead evaluation will provide the primary comparison because the historical series are relatively short.

Where sufficient observations are available, additional forecast horizons may be examined to assess the stability of longer-term extrapolations.

The evaluation will distinguish between the longer overall HPC and CPU series covering 2014–2026 and the shorter GPU modelling series covering 2022 - 2026.

For GPU demand, the small number of recent observations severely limits the number of meaningful cross-validation origins. Any accuracy comparisons will therefore be considered exploratory.

### Forecast Accuracy Measures

Forecast errors will be evaluated on the original scale of each demand measure using:

1. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), which gives greater weight to larger forecast errors.
2. Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which measures the average absolute forecast error.

Accuracy measures will be calculated separately for overall HPC, CPU, and GPU demand because their units are different.

For log-linear models, predictions will be back-transformed to the original demand scale before calculating forecast errors. The evaluation will distinguish exponentiated log-scale predictions from bias-adjusted mean forecasts where relevant.

Models will be compared using common evaluation origins within each demand measure to ensure a fair comparison.

### Model Selection Principles

The preferred forecasting approach will not be selected solely on the basis of the lowest numerical error.

Model selection will also consider:

- Stability across evaluation periods and forecast origins.
- Sensitivity to the inclusion of the 2026 observation.
- Residual behaviour and evidence of systematic model errors.
- Interpretability and consistency with historical data provenance.
- The risks of extrapolating growth patterns through 2030.

Particular caution will be applied to GPU demand because its historical modelling period is short and includes estimates derived from different reporting and conversion methods.

The evaluation results presented in this chapter will provide the statistical evidence for model selection in Chapter 09 and the development of demand scenarios in Chapter 10.

## Rolling-Origin Cross-Validation

### Overall HPC Demand: One-Step-Ahead Evaluation

The initial evaluation focuses on overall HPC demand, measured in thousands of service units (kSU).

Rolling-origin cross-validation is applied using an initial training period of 2014 - 2020. At each subsequent origin, the training sample expands by one year, and a one-step-ahead forecast is generated for the following year.

This produces six evaluation years, from 2021 to 2026.

Four forecasting approaches are evaluated using identical training periods and forecast origins:

1. Naïve benchmark.
2. Drift benchmark.
3. Linear trend.
4. Log-linear trend.

Each model is re-estimated using only the observations available at the corresponding forecast origin. This avoids information leakage from future observations.

```{r overall-cv-predictions}

  dplyr::transmute(
    year = as.integer(year),
    demand_ksu = as.numeric(demand_ksu)

  dplyr::arrange(year)

  identical(overall_cv_data$year, 2014:2026),
  all(is.finite(overall_cv_data$demand_ksu)),
  all(overall_cv_data$demand_ksu > 0)

# Define common one-step-ahead evaluation years
cv_test_years <- 2021:2026

# Generate forecasts using expanding training windows
overall_cv_predictions <- dplyr::bind_rows(
  lapply(cv_test_years, function(test_year) {

    training <- overall_cv_data |>
      dplyr::filter(year < test_year) |>
      dplyr::mutate(time = year - 2014)

    actual <- overall_cv_data$demand_ksu[
      overall_cv_data$year == test_year
    ]

    future <- data.frame(time = test_year - 2014)

    # Naïve: last observed demand
    naive_forecast <- tail(training$demand_ksu, 1)

    # Drift: average change from first to last observation
    drift_forecast <- tail(training$demand_ksu, 1) +
      (
        tail(training$demand_ksu, 1) -
          training$demand_ksu[1]
      ) / (nrow(training) - 1)

    # Linear trend fitted only to training data
    linear_fit <- lm(
      demand_ksu ~ time,
      data = training
    )

    linear_forecast <- as.numeric(
      predict(linear_fit, newdata = future)
    )

    # Log-linear trend fitted only to training data
    loglinear_fit <- lm(
      log(demand_ksu) ~ time,
      data = training
    )

    loglinear_forecast <- exp(as.numeric(
      predict(loglinear_fit, newdata = future)
    ))

    data.frame(
      year = test_year,
      actual = actual,
      naive = naive_forecast,
      drift = drift_forecast,
      linear = linear_forecast,
      loglinear = loglinear_forecast
    )
  })
)
```

```{r cpu-cv-predictions}
      data = training
    )

      predict(loglinear_fit, newdata = future)
    ))

      year = test_year,
      actual = actual,
```

## Field of Research Allocation-Share Evaluation

### Purpose and Evaluation Approach

The Field of Research (FoR) evaluation examines the historical distribution and stability of HPC resource allocations across research fields.

Unlike the overall HPC, CPU, and GPU evaluations, this analysis does not assess independently observed FoR-specific researcher demand forecasts.

Instead, it evaluates whether historical allocation shares provide a sufficiently consistent basis for developing indicative FoR demand scenarios through 2030.

The analysis focuses on 2021–2026, corresponding to the FoR 2020 classification period used in Chapter 06.

Three considerations guide the evaluation:

1. Historical stability of FoR allocation shares.
2. Variation between the latest-year composition and the recent-period average.
3. Sensitivity to classification, reporting coverage, and allocation-based measurement assumptions.

The results will inform the selection of FoR share assumptions in Chapter 09 and their application to overall HPC demand forecasts in Chapter 10.

### FoR Evaluation Findings and Limitations

The Field of Research (FoR) evaluation provides evidence on the historical composition and variability of HPC resource allocations across research fields. Unlike the overall HPC, CPU, and GPU evaluations, which assess forecasting performance against historical resource measures, the FoR evaluation is descriptive. Its purpose is to assess the stability and comparability of allocation shares rather than to establish predictive accuracy for research-field demand.

**Historical allocation-share patterns**

The 2021 - 2026 evaluation shows that Engineering & Technology accounted for the largest average allocation share (34.88%), followed by Physical Sciences (22.13%), Chemical Sciences (17.84%), and Earth & Environmental Sciences (14.00%). These categories were observed in all six years, although their shares varied over time.

The longer 2014 - 2026 evaluation indicates that allocation composition was not constant across the historical period. Engineering & Technology and Earth & Environmental Sciences each exhibited a range of 29.65 percentage points, while Physical Sciences exhibited a range of 26.63 percentage points. Chemical Sciences was comparatively stable, with a range of 4.69 percentage points.

The separate 2014 - 2020 evaluation provides additional context. Earth & Environmental Sciences accounted for the largest average allocation share (31.62%), followed by Chemical Sciences (19.02%), Physical Sciences (16.92%), and Engineering & Technology (16.90%). Several categories exhibited substantial variation during this earlier period.

**Differences between reporting periods**

Comparing the two periods using harmonised analytical categories reveals substantial differences in average allocation composition. Engineering & Technology increased from 16.90% in 2014 - 2020 to 34.88% in 2021 - 2026, a difference of +17.98 percentage points. Conversely, Earth & Environmental Sciences decreased from 31.62% to 14.00%, a difference of -17.61 percentage points. Physical Sciences recorded a higher average share (+5.21 percentage points), while Mathematics & Computing recorded a lower average (-2.46 percentage points).

These differences indicate changes in the recorded distribution of HPC allocations across research fields. They do not, however, establish equivalent changes in underlying researcher demand.

**Data and interpretation limitations**

Several limitations affect the interpretation of these findings:

1. **Allocation versus demand:** The FoR dataset represents allocated HPC resources, not directly observed research-field requests or unconstrained computational needs. Allocation shares may therefore reflect resource availability, allocation decisions, and reporting practices as well as researcher activity.

2. **Classification comparability:** The historical analysis spans FoR 2008 and FoR 2020 reporting frameworks. Although the categories were harmonised for analytical purposes in Chapter 06, they are not necessarily equivalent to official classifications across all years.

3. **Incomplete category coverage:** Not all research-field groups are observed in every year. Missing observations were not automatically interpreted as zero allocations. Consequently, averages and ranges for categories with limited coverage are less informative than those for consistently reported categories.

4. **Reporting scope:** Historical differences in facility coverage, resource accounting, and reporting conventions may influence the recorded allocation shares. Apparent changes in research-field composition cannot be attributed exclusively to changes in research priorities.

5. **Period-average interpretation:** The comparison uses unweighted averages of available annual allocation shares. These describe the typical recorded composition within each period but do not represent shares of the total HPC resources allocated across all years combined.

6. **Limited predictive validation:** The available FoR allocation-share series does not provide a consistent, directly observed research-field demand target suitable for evaluating field-specific demand forecasts using the same rolling-origin procedure applied to the overall HPC, CPU, and GPU series.

**Implications for model selection and scenarios**

The FoR evaluation supports the use of historical allocation composition as a reference for developing research-field scenarios, but not as a direct forecast of field-specific researcher demand.

The more recent 2021 - 2026 shares provide a relevant reference for a baseline allocation-composition assumption, subject to their observed variability and reporting limitations. The earlier period and the full historical series provide additional context for examining alternative compositions and assessing sensitivity to changes in research-field allocation patterns.

Accordingly, Chapter 09 will distinguish models selected through quantitative forecast-error evaluation from FoR assumptions supported by descriptive historical evidence. Any subsequent application of FoR shares to projected overall HPC demand will be presented as a scenario-based allocation of projected demand, rather than an independently validated forecast of demand within each research field.

## Overall Evaluation Conclusions and Forecast-Horizon Limitations

The rolling-origin evaluation provides a consistent basis for comparing candidate forecasting models using historical one-step-ahead prediction errors. For overall HPC and CPU demand, six forecast errors are available for 2021–2026. For GPU demand, only two forecast errors are available for 2025–2026.

The results support the selection of a linear trend as the central forecasting approach for overall HPC and CPU demand. However, the evaluation does not directly establish the accuracy of forecasts two, three, or four years ahead. The relative performance of alternative models may change over longer horizons.

The GPU evaluation is particularly limited by its short historical series. Although the naïve benchmark performs best in the available one-step-ahead comparison, this does not establish that GPU demand will remain constant through 2030.

The FoR analysis evaluates historical allocation-share composition and variability rather than independently testing future demand forecasts by research field.

Consequently, the 2027–2030 projections should be interpreted as planning scenarios informed by historical predictive performance, not as forecasts with independently validated four-year-ahead accuracy. The alternative growth specifications, 2026-exclusion sensitivity analyses, and conditional prediction intervals in Chapter 10 provide additional perspectives on uncertainty but do not replace direct multi-horizon validation.
