11.1 Purpose and Scope

This chapter develops indicative scenarios for Australian national research high-performance computing (HPC) demand through 2030, drawing on the forecasting models evaluated in Chapter 08 and selected in Chapter 09.

The analysis considers four dimensions:

  1. Overall HPC demand, expressed in kSU.
  2. CPU demand, expressed in million CPU core-hours.
  3. GPU demand, expressed in million GPU-hours.
  4. Field of Research (FoR) allocation-share-based distributions of projected overall HPC demand.

The projection period covers 2027–2030, with 2026 representing the latest available historical observation.

The objective is to provide transparent, reproducible estimates of potential future computational requirements that can inform infrastructure planning and policy discussions.

The scenarios are conditional on the available historical data, model specifications, and stated assumptions. They should not be interpreted as definitive predictions of unconstrained national HPC demand.

11.2 Scenario Framework

The scenario framework distinguishes central statistical projections, historical sensitivity comparisons, and exploratory growth trajectories.

Demand measure Reference approach Alternative assumptions
Overall HPC Full-history linear trend Linear trend excluding 2026; log-linear growth
CPU Full-history linear trend Linear trend excluding 2026; log-linear growth
GPU Naïve statistical benchmark Linear and log-linear exploratory trajectories; sensitivity to 2026
FoR Recent-period allocation composition Latest-year and earlier-period allocation compositions

For overall HPC and CPU demand, the full-history linear trend models provide the central statistical projections because they demonstrated the strongest or most competitive historical forecasting performance.

The 2026-excluded linear specifications are retained to examine the sensitivity of projected demand to the latest observation.

Log-linear models are retained to illustrate the implications of compounded proportional growth. These projections are alternative modelling assumptions rather than statistically validated upper bounds.

For GPU demand, the naïve model serves as the statistical reference benchmark. Linear and log-linear projections are presented as exploratory trajectories because the available historical series is short and the rolling-origin evaluation contains only two test years.

FoR scenarios use historical allocation compositions to distribute projected overall HPC demand across harmonised research categories. These distributions are conditional allocation-share scenarios, not independently validated FoR-specific demand forecasts.

11.3 Interpretation of Scenario Uncertainty

Three sources of uncertainty are considered throughout the chapter:

  • Measurement uncertainty: Differences in historical reporting, resource accounting, and conversion assumptions.
  • Model uncertainty: Differences in forecasting specifications and their underlying growth assumptions.
  • Scenario uncertainty: Differences in assumed future growth and allocation composition.

The scenarios are not assigned probabilities, and the differences between model projections should not be interpreted as formal prediction intervals.

All numerical results will retain their original resource units. Overall HPC kSU, CPU core-hours, and GPU-hours will be analysed separately rather than combined without a justified conversion framework.

The analysis prioritises transparency, reproducibility, and clear communication of uncertainty over unnecessary model complexity.

11.4 Overall HPC Demand Scenarios

11.4.1 Forecast Construction

Overall HPC demand projections are generated for 2027–2030 using the linear and log-linear models developed in Chapter 07 and evaluated in Chapter 08.

The full-history linear trend is adopted as the central statistical projection, consistent with the model-selection decision in Chapter 09.

A linear trend excluding 2026 is retained to examine sensitivity to the latest historical observation. Full-history and 2026-excluded log-linear specifications are also presented to illustrate alternative compounded-growth assumptions.

All projections are expressed in kSU and represent model-based estimates of the constructed historical demand measure, rather than forecasts of allocated computing capacity.

11.4.4 Scenario Results and Interpretation

The overall HPC demand scenarios indicate continued growth in national research computing requirements through 2030, although the projected magnitude depends substantially on the selected model specification.

Central linear projection

The selected full-history linear model projects overall HPC demand increasing from approximately 2.017 million kSU in 2027 to 2.503 million kSU in 2030.

This corresponds to an estimated annual absolute increase of approximately 161,992 kSU, consistent with the fitted historical trend.

The central projection is preferred because the linear model achieved the lowest rolling-origin RMSE and MAE among the evaluated overall HPC models in Chapter 08.

However, this result reflects historical one-step-ahead predictive performance and does not guarantee equivalent accuracy over the full projection horizon.

Sensitivity to the 2026 observation

Excluding the 2026 observation reduces the projected 2030 demand from 2,502,822 kSU to 2,285,327 kSU.

The difference is approximately 217,495 kSU, equivalent to 8.7% of the central projection.

This demonstrates that the latest observation materially influences the estimated linear growth trajectory.

The sensitivity comparison does not establish whether the 2026 increase represents a sustained change in researcher demand, a temporary development, or differences in historical reporting.

Alternative compounded-growth trajectories

The full-history log-linear model projects approximately 6.483 million kSU in 2030, while the model excluding 2026 projects approximately 6.963 million kSU.

These values are substantially higher than the central linear projection because the log-linear specifications extrapolate compounded proportional growth rather than a constant annual absolute increase.

However, Chapter 08 found that the log-linear model had substantially higher rolling-origin forecast errors than the linear model.

The log-linear projections are therefore retained as exploratory alternative growth trajectories rather than equally supported central forecasts or statistically validated upper demand bounds.

Notably, excluding 2026 increases the log-linear projection, despite reducing the corresponding linear projection. This illustrates how sensitivity to historical observations depends on the model specification.

Implications for infrastructure planning

The central statistical projection suggests that national research HPC requirements could reach approximately 2.50 million kSU by 2030 if the fitted historical linear trend continues.

The sensitivity analysis indicates that the projected magnitude depends partly on the interpretation of the latest historical observation.

The alternative log-linear trajectories demonstrate how substantially greater requirements could emerge under continued compounded growth, although the available historical evaluation provides weaker support for these specifications.

These results highlight the importance of maintaining flexibility in infrastructure planning and distinguishing projected researcher requirements from actual allocated computing capacity.

The scenarios should be interpreted as conditional, model-based estimates rather than definitive predictions of future national HPC demand.

11.5.4 Assessment of Prediction Interval Assumptions

The 80% and 95% prediction intervals presented for overall HPC and CPU demand are derived from the selected full-history linear regression models using conventional prediction interval calculations.

Their interpretation depends on the adequacy of the fitted models and their underlying error assumptions, including independence, approximately constant variance, and normality.

Residual diagnostics were examined for both selected models using residual-versus-fitted plots, residuals over time, normal Q–Q plots, and exploratory Ljung–Box tests.

The Ljung–Box tests at lag 2 returned p-values of 0.8775 for overall HPC and 0.3608 for CPU. Neither test detected statistically significant residual autocorrelation at the 5% significance level.

However, the limited sample of 13 annual observations reduces the power of these tests. Non-significant results should therefore not be interpreted as confirmation of residual independence.

For overall HPC, the residuals fluctuate around zero, with a relatively large positive residual in 2026. The normal Q–Q plot shows approximate alignment with the reference line but some departures at the distribution tails.

For CPU, the residuals also fluctuate around zero, although several years exhibit substantial positive or negative deviations. The Q–Q plot shows departures from the normal reference line, particularly in the lower tail.

The residual plots do not establish a definitive pattern of changing variance, but the available evidence is insufficient to confirm homoscedasticity or normality.

Accordingly, the reported 80% and 95% prediction intervals are interpreted as conditional, model-based uncertainty estimates rather than empirically validated coverage bounds.

These intervals reflect uncertainty under the selected linear regression specifications. They do not incorporate uncertainty arising from historical measurement differences, alternative model selection, future structural changes, or shifts in research computing policy and infrastructure.

The alternative log-linear trajectories and 2026 sensitivity analyses should therefore be considered alongside the prediction intervals when assessing possible future computational requirements.

The prediction intervals provide an additional perspective on statistical uncertainty, but they do not represent comprehensive lower and upper limits for national HPC or CPU demand through 2030.

11.6 GPU Demand Scenarios

11.6.1 Forecast Construction and Limitations

GPU demand scenarios are developed for 2027–2030 using the recent 2022–2026 historical series, expressed in million GPU-hours.

Unlike the overall HPC and CPU analyses, the GPU series contains only five annual estimates, combining reported Setonix-based demand with estimates derived from GPU allocation shares and working conversion assumptions.

Chapter 08 found that the naïve benchmark achieved the lowest rolling-origin RMSE and MAE among the evaluated GPU models. However, the evaluation covered only two forecast years, providing insufficient evidence to establish a definitive long-term forecasting model.

Accordingly, the naïve approach is retained as a constant-demand statistical benchmark. Linear and log-linear specifications, fitted both with and without the 2026 observation, are retained as exploratory growth trajectories.

The historical GPU conversion scenarios for 2014–2021 are not included as additional observations in these forecasting models because they represent hypothetical conversion assumptions rather than independently observed GPU demand.

The resulting projections should be interpreted as conditional scenarios rather than validated long-term forecasts.

11.6.3 GPU Scenario Results and Interpretation

The GPU demand scenarios produce substantially different trajectories for 2027–2030, reflecting both alternative growth assumptions and the influence of the 2026 observation.

The naïve benchmark maintains the 2026 GPU demand estimate of approximately 3.003 million GPU-hours throughout the forecast horizon. Although this benchmark achieved the lowest rolling-origin RMSE and MAE in Chapter 08, its evaluation was based on only two forecast years. Its relatively favourable short-term performance therefore does not establish that GPU demand will remain constant through 2030.

Under the full-history linear specification, projected GPU demand increases from approximately 2.855 million GPU-hours in 2027 to 3.371 million GPU-hours in 2030. The corresponding full-history log-linear scenario reaches approximately 3.681 million GPU-hours by 2030, reflecting the assumption of compounded proportional growth.

Excluding 2026 produces considerably lower projections. The 2030 linear projection falls to approximately 2.252 million GPU-hours, while the corresponding log-linear projection reaches approximately 2.406 million GPU-hours.

The difference between the full-history and 2026-excluded linear projections is approximately 1.119 million GPU-hours in 2030, equivalent to an increase of approximately 49.7% relative to the 2026-excluded projection.

Across the four exploratory trend specifications, projected GPU demand in 2030 ranges from approximately 2.252 to 3.681 million GPU-hours. Including the naïve benchmark does not change these minimum and maximum values.

This range should not be interpreted as a statistical prediction interval or as validated lower and upper limits for future GPU demand. It represents the spread of projections generated by the selected modelling assumptions.

The substantial differences between scenarios are particularly important given the limitations of the historical GPU series. Only five annual estimates are available for 2022–2026, combining reported Setonix-based demand with estimates derived from allocation shares and working conversion assumptions.

Consequently, the available evidence is insufficient to establish a definitive central GPU demand forecast. The naïve benchmark provides a constant-demand reference, while the linear and log-linear specifications illustrate alternative growth trajectories.

Unlike the overall HPC and CPU analyses, formal 80% and 95% prediction intervals are not reported for GPU demand. With such a short and heterogeneous historical series, conventional regression intervals would be highly sensitive to modelling assumptions and difficult to interpret as reliable coverage bounds.

For national research computing planning, these results indicate that GPU demand should be considered through multiple conditional scenarios rather than a single point estimate. Improved GPU-specific demand reporting, consistent measurement units, and additional annual observations would strengthen future forecasting and infrastructure planning.

The GPU scenarios should therefore be interpreted as exploratory planning inputs, not independently validated forecasts of national GPU requirements.

11.7 Field of Research Allocation-Share Scenarios

11.7.1 Purpose and Methodology

The Field of Research (FoR) scenarios examine how projected overall HPC demand for 2027–2030 could be distributed across research fields under alternative historical allocation-share assumptions.

These scenarios use the harmonised FoR allocation shares developed in Chapter 06 and the central overall HPC linear projection developed earlier in this chapter.

The analysis adopts a top-down approach:

[ {f,t} = {t} _{f} ]

where (f) denotes the research-field group and (t) denotes the forecast year.

The resulting values are expressed in kSU. They represent hypothetical distributions of projected overall demand, not independently estimated or observed FoR-level researcher demand.

Two historical reference periods are considered:

  • Recent-period composition (2021–2026): Uses the harmonised FoR 2020 classification groups to reflect more recent allocation patterns.
  • Longer-term composition (2014–2026): Uses the harmonised analytical FoR groups to examine historical allocation patterns across the wider period.

The two classification structures are not assumed to be directly interchangeable. Harmonisation supports broad analytical comparisons but does not establish complete equivalence between official FoR classification versions.

Annual allocation shares are examined before constructing scenario assumptions. Category coverage, reporting scope, and changes in classification are retained as methodological limitations.

In particular, the 2017 FoR observation requires caution because its reporting scope differs from other years.

The scenarios hold assumed research-field shares constant over 2027–2030. This is a transparent planning assumption rather than evidence that future disciplinary demand composition will remain unchanged.

11.7.8 Comparison and Interpretation of FoR Scenarios

The three FoR allocation-share scenarios illustrate how the distribution of projected overall HPC demand varies under different historical reference-period assumptions.

All scenarios apply their respective normalised historical allocation-share weights to the same central overall HPC demand projection. Consequently, differences between the scenarios reflect the assumed disciplinary composition rather than differences in the total national demand forecast.

Under the earlier-period assumptions (2014–2020), Earth & Environmental Sciences receives the largest illustrative share at 30.38%, followed by Chemical Sciences (18.28%), Physical Sciences (16.26%), and Engineering & Technology (16.24%).

Under the recent-period assumptions (2021–2026), Engineering & Technology becomes the largest group at 33.28%, followed by Physical Sciences (21.11%), Chemical Sciences (17.02%), and Earth & Environmental Sciences (13.36%).

The shift in normalised scenario weights is substantial for the two largest changing groups. Engineering & Technology increases by approximately 17.04 percentage points between the earlier and recent reference periods, while Earth & Environmental Sciences decreases by approximately 17.02 percentage points.

The longer-term scenario (2014–2026) provides a third reference. Engineering & Technology accounts for 24.19%, Earth & Environmental Sciences for 22.55%, Physical Sciences for 18.55%, and Chemical Sciences for 17.74%.

These differences illustrate the sensitivity of FoR-level planning allocations to the selected historical reference period.

However, they should not automatically be interpreted as changes in underlying researcher demand. The historical data span different FoR classification structures, and differences in facility coverage, reporting scope, category completeness, and allocation practices may contribute to the observed variation.

In addition, several categories are not reported consistently across all years. The scenario weights were constructed by normalising observed-year mean allocation shares to sum to 100%, without treating unreported category-years as confirmed zero allocations.

The resulting FoR projections therefore represent conditional distributions of projected overall HPC demand, not independently validated demand forecasts for individual research fields.

The earlier-period, recent-period, and longer-term scenarios provide complementary planning perspectives. Their differences indicate that infrastructure planning should consider uncertainty in disciplinary composition rather than relying exclusively on a single historical allocation pattern.

The analysis does not establish which historical composition will best represent future demand. Further work would require consistently classified FoR-level demand requests, comparable facility reporting, and improved coverage of currently incomplete categories.

# Demand Scenarios: 2027–2030

## Purpose and Scope

This chapter develops indicative scenarios for Australian national research high-performance computing (HPC) demand through 2030, drawing on the forecasting models evaluated in Chapter 08 and selected in Chapter 09.

The analysis considers four dimensions:

1. Overall HPC demand, expressed in kSU.
2. CPU demand, expressed in million CPU core-hours.
3. GPU demand, expressed in million GPU-hours.
4. Field of Research (FoR) allocation-share-based distributions of projected overall HPC demand.

The projection period covers 2027–2030, with 2026 representing the latest available historical observation.

The objective is to provide transparent, reproducible estimates of potential future computational requirements that can inform infrastructure planning and policy discussions.

The scenarios are conditional on the available historical data, model specifications, and stated assumptions. They should not be interpreted as definitive predictions of unconstrained national HPC demand.

## Scenario Framework

The scenario framework distinguishes central statistical projections, historical sensitivity comparisons, and exploratory growth trajectories.

| Demand measure | Reference approach | Alternative assumptions |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Overall HPC | Full-history linear trend | Linear trend excluding 2026; log-linear growth |
| CPU | Full-history linear trend | Linear trend excluding 2026; log-linear growth |
| GPU | Naïve statistical benchmark | Linear and log-linear exploratory trajectories; sensitivity to 2026 |
| FoR | Recent-period allocation composition | Latest-year and earlier-period allocation compositions |

For overall HPC and CPU demand, the full-history linear trend models provide the central statistical projections because they demonstrated the strongest or most competitive historical forecasting performance.

The 2026-excluded linear specifications are retained to examine the sensitivity of projected demand to the latest observation.

Log-linear models are retained to illustrate the implications of compounded proportional growth. These projections are alternative modelling assumptions rather than statistically validated upper bounds.

For GPU demand, the naïve model serves as the statistical reference benchmark. Linear and log-linear projections are presented as exploratory trajectories because the available historical series is short and the rolling-origin evaluation contains only two test years.

FoR scenarios use historical allocation compositions to distribute projected overall HPC demand across harmonised research categories. These distributions are conditional allocation-share scenarios, not independently validated FoR-specific demand forecasts.

## Interpretation of Scenario Uncertainty

Three sources of uncertainty are considered throughout the chapter:

- **Measurement uncertainty:** Differences in historical reporting, resource accounting, and conversion assumptions.
- **Model uncertainty:** Differences in forecasting specifications and their underlying growth assumptions.
- **Scenario uncertainty:** Differences in assumed future growth and allocation composition.

The scenarios are not assigned probabilities, and the differences between model projections should not be interpreted as formal prediction intervals.

All numerical results will retain their original resource units. Overall HPC kSU, CPU core-hours, and GPU-hours will be analysed separately rather than combined without a justified conversion framework.

The analysis prioritises transparency, reproducibility, and clear communication of uncertainty over unnecessary model complexity.

### Scenario Results and Interpretation

The overall HPC demand scenarios indicate continued growth in national research computing requirements through 2030, although the projected magnitude depends substantially on the selected model specification.

**Central linear projection**

The selected full-history linear model projects overall HPC demand increasing from approximately 2.017 million kSU in 2027 to 2.503 million kSU in 2030.

This corresponds to an estimated annual absolute increase of approximately 161,992 kSU, consistent with the fitted historical trend.

The central projection is preferred because the linear model achieved the lowest rolling-origin RMSE and MAE among the evaluated overall HPC models in Chapter 08.

However, this result reflects historical one-step-ahead predictive performance and does not guarantee equivalent accuracy over the full projection horizon.

**Sensitivity to the 2026 observation**

Excluding the 2026 observation reduces the projected 2030 demand from 2,502,822 kSU to 2,285,327 kSU.

The difference is approximately 217,495 kSU, equivalent to 8.7% of the central projection.

This demonstrates that the latest observation materially influences the estimated linear growth trajectory.

The sensitivity comparison does not establish whether the 2026 increase represents a sustained change in researcher demand, a temporary development, or differences in historical reporting.

**Alternative compounded-growth trajectories**

The full-history log-linear model projects approximately 6.483 million kSU in 2030, while the model excluding 2026 projects approximately 6.963 million kSU.

These values are substantially higher than the central linear projection because the log-linear specifications extrapolate compounded proportional growth rather than a constant annual absolute increase.

However, Chapter 08 found that the log-linear model had substantially higher rolling-origin forecast errors than the linear model.

The log-linear projections are therefore retained as exploratory alternative growth trajectories rather than equally supported central forecasts or statistically validated upper demand bounds.

Notably, excluding 2026 increases the log-linear projection, despite reducing the corresponding linear projection. This illustrates how sensitivity to historical observations depends on the model specification.

**Implications for infrastructure planning**

The central statistical projection suggests that national research HPC requirements could reach approximately 2.50 million kSU by 2030 if the fitted historical linear trend continues.

The sensitivity analysis indicates that the projected magnitude depends partly on the interpretation of the latest historical observation.

The alternative log-linear trajectories demonstrate how substantially greater requirements could emerge under continued compounded growth, although the available historical evaluation provides weaker support for these specifications.

These results highlight the importance of maintaining flexibility in infrastructure planning and distinguishing projected researcher requirements from actual allocated computing capacity.

The scenarios should be interpreted as conditional, model-based estimates rather than definitive predictions of future national HPC demand.

### Assessment of Prediction Interval Assumptions

The 80% and 95% prediction intervals presented for overall HPC and CPU demand are derived from the selected full-history linear regression models using conventional prediction interval calculations.

Their interpretation depends on the adequacy of the fitted models and their underlying error assumptions, including independence, approximately constant variance, and normality.

Residual diagnostics were examined for both selected models using residual-versus-fitted plots, residuals over time, normal Q–Q plots, and exploratory Ljung–Box tests.

The Ljung–Box tests at lag 2 returned p-values of 0.8775 for overall HPC and 0.3608 for CPU. Neither test detected statistically significant residual autocorrelation at the 5% significance level.

However, the limited sample of 13 annual observations reduces the power of these tests. Non-significant results should therefore not be interpreted as confirmation of residual independence.

For overall HPC, the residuals fluctuate around zero, with a relatively large positive residual in 2026. The normal Q–Q plot shows approximate alignment with the reference line but some departures at the distribution tails.

For CPU, the residuals also fluctuate around zero, although several years exhibit substantial positive or negative deviations. The Q–Q plot shows departures from the normal reference line, particularly in the lower tail.

The residual plots do not establish a definitive pattern of changing variance, but the available evidence is insufficient to confirm homoscedasticity or normality.

Accordingly, the reported 80% and 95% prediction intervals are interpreted as **conditional, model-based uncertainty estimates** rather than empirically validated coverage bounds.

These intervals reflect uncertainty under the selected linear regression specifications. They do not incorporate uncertainty arising from historical measurement differences, alternative model selection, future structural changes, or shifts in research computing policy and infrastructure.

The alternative log-linear trajectories and 2026 sensitivity analyses should therefore be considered alongside the prediction intervals when assessing possible future computational requirements.

The prediction intervals provide an additional perspective on statistical uncertainty, but they do not represent comprehensive lower and upper limits for national HPC or CPU demand through 2030.

### GPU Scenario Results and Interpretation

The GPU demand scenarios produce substantially different trajectories for 2027–2030, reflecting both alternative growth assumptions and the influence of the 2026 observation.

The naïve benchmark maintains the 2026 GPU demand estimate of approximately 3.003 million GPU-hours throughout the forecast horizon. Although this benchmark achieved the lowest rolling-origin RMSE and MAE in Chapter 08, its evaluation was based on only two forecast years. Its relatively favourable short-term performance therefore does not establish that GPU demand will remain constant through 2030.

Under the full-history linear specification, projected GPU demand increases from approximately 2.855 million GPU-hours in 2027 to 3.371 million GPU-hours in 2030. The corresponding full-history log-linear scenario reaches approximately 3.681 million GPU-hours by 2030, reflecting the assumption of compounded proportional growth.

Excluding 2026 produces considerably lower projections. The 2030 linear projection falls to approximately 2.252 million GPU-hours, while the corresponding log-linear projection reaches approximately 2.406 million GPU-hours.

The difference between the full-history and 2026-excluded linear projections is approximately 1.119 million GPU-hours in 2030, equivalent to an increase of approximately 49.7% relative to the 2026-excluded projection.

Across the four exploratory trend specifications, projected GPU demand in 2030 ranges from approximately **2.252 to 3.681 million GPU-hours**. Including the naïve benchmark does not change these minimum and maximum values.

This range should not be interpreted as a statistical prediction interval or as validated lower and upper limits for future GPU demand. It represents the spread of projections generated by the selected modelling assumptions.

The substantial differences between scenarios are particularly important given the limitations of the historical GPU series. Only five annual estimates are available for 2022–2026, combining reported Setonix-based demand with estimates derived from allocation shares and working conversion assumptions.

Consequently, the available evidence is insufficient to establish a definitive central GPU demand forecast. The naïve benchmark provides a constant-demand reference, while the linear and log-linear specifications illustrate alternative growth trajectories.

Unlike the overall HPC and CPU analyses, formal 80% and 95% prediction intervals are not reported for GPU demand. With such a short and heterogeneous historical series, conventional regression intervals would be highly sensitive to modelling assumptions and difficult to interpret as reliable coverage bounds.

For national research computing planning, these results indicate that GPU demand should be considered through multiple conditional scenarios rather than a single point estimate. Improved GPU-specific demand reporting, consistent measurement units, and additional annual observations would strengthen future forecasting and infrastructure planning.

The GPU scenarios should therefore be interpreted as **exploratory planning inputs**, not independently validated forecasts of national GPU requirements.


## Field of Research Allocation-Share Scenarios

### Purpose and Methodology

The Field of Research (FoR) scenarios examine how projected overall HPC demand for 2027–2030 could be distributed across research fields under alternative historical allocation-share assumptions.

These scenarios use the harmonised FoR allocation shares developed in Chapter 06 and the central overall HPC linear projection developed earlier in this chapter.

The analysis adopts a top-down approach:

\[
\text{Illustrative FoR demand}_{f,t}
=
\text{Projected overall HPC demand}_{t}
\times
\text{Assumed allocation share}_{f}
\]

where \(f\) denotes the research-field group and \(t\) denotes the forecast year.

The resulting values are expressed in kSU. They represent hypothetical distributions of projected overall demand, **not independently estimated or observed FoR-level researcher demand**.

Two historical reference periods are considered:

- **Recent-period composition (2021–2026):** Uses the harmonised FoR 2020 classification groups to reflect more recent allocation patterns.
- **Longer-term composition (2014–2026):** Uses the harmonised analytical FoR groups to examine historical allocation patterns across the wider period.

The two classification structures are not assumed to be directly interchangeable. Harmonisation supports broad analytical comparisons but does not establish complete equivalence between official FoR classification versions.

Annual allocation shares are examined before constructing scenario assumptions. Category coverage, reporting scope, and changes in classification are retained as methodological limitations.

In particular, the 2017 FoR observation requires caution because its reporting scope differs from other years.

The scenarios hold assumed research-field shares constant over 2027–2030. This is a transparent planning assumption rather than evidence that future disciplinary demand composition will remain unchanged.

The three FoR allocation-share scenarios illustrate how the distribution of projected overall HPC demand varies under different historical reference-period assumptions.

All scenarios apply their respective normalised historical allocation-share weights to the same central overall HPC demand projection. Consequently, differences between the scenarios reflect the assumed disciplinary composition rather than differences in the total national demand forecast.

Under the earlier-period assumptions (2014–2020), Earth & Environmental Sciences receives the largest illustrative share at 30.38%, followed by Chemical Sciences (18.28%), Physical Sciences (16.26%), and Engineering & Technology (16.24%).

Under the recent-period assumptions (2021–2026), Engineering & Technology becomes the largest group at 33.28%, followed by Physical Sciences (21.11%), Chemical Sciences (17.02%), and Earth & Environmental Sciences (13.36%).

The shift in normalised scenario weights is substantial for the two largest changing groups. Engineering & Technology increases by approximately 17.04 percentage points between the earlier and recent reference periods, while Earth & Environmental Sciences decreases by approximately 17.02 percentage points.

The longer-term scenario (2014–2026) provides a third reference. Engineering & Technology accounts for 24.19%, Earth & Environmental Sciences for 22.55%, Physical Sciences for 18.55%, and Chemical Sciences for 17.74%.

These differences illustrate the sensitivity of FoR-level planning allocations to the selected historical reference period.

However, they should not automatically be interpreted as changes in underlying researcher demand. The historical data span different FoR classification structures, and differences in facility coverage, reporting scope, category completeness, and allocation practices may contribute to the observed variation.

In addition, several categories are not reported consistently across all years. The scenario weights were constructed by normalising observed-year mean allocation shares to sum to 100%, without treating unreported category-years as confirmed zero allocations.

The resulting FoR projections therefore represent conditional distributions of projected overall HPC demand, not independently validated demand forecasts for individual research fields.

The earlier-period, recent-period, and longer-term scenarios provide complementary planning perspectives. Their differences indicate that infrastructure planning should consider uncertainty in disciplinary composition rather than relying exclusively on a single historical allocation pattern.

The analysis does not establish which historical composition will best represent future demand. Further work would require consistently classified FoR-level demand requests, comparable facility reporting, and improved coverage of currently incomplete categories.
